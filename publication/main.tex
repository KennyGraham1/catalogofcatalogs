\begin{abstract}
Earthquake catalogs are essential for seismology, underpinning research, monitoring, and risk management. In Aotearoa New Zealand, catalogs are developed for diverse purposes, but suffer from uneven access, inconsistent standards, and limited interoperability. Two national workshops in 2023 and 2024 brought together researchers, agencies, and industry to co-design a practical framework guided by the FAIR principles (Findability, Accessibility, Interoperability, and Reusability) and, in the Aotearoa New Zealand context, by the CARE Principles for Indigenous Data Governance (Collective benefit, Authority to control, Responsibility, and Ethics). This white paper outlines the current catalog landscape, community-identified challenges, and a proposed framework with implementation recommendations. A prototype portal has been developed to exercise the framework specifications against representative datasets, demonstrating end-to-end workflows for catalog ingestion, quality assessment, merge and conflation, analytics, and programmatic access. Our aim is to establish a national capability that improves transparency, comparability, and reusability of earthquake data, enabling reproducible science, accelerating research, and strengthening hazard and risk decision-making.
\end{abstract}

\bigskip
\noindent{\color{midnightgreen}\large\bfseries Key Recommendations}
\begin{enumerate}
\setlength{\itemsep}{1pt}
\item Adopt a shared catalog metadata profile and controlled parameter lexicon aligned with QuakeML and FDSN conventions, distinguishing required from recommended fields.
\item Require persistent identifiers (DOIs), explicit versioning, and machine-readable changelogs so that any cited catalog version can be retrieved exactly as used.
\item Establish a national ``catalog of catalogs'' portal and API --- complementing, not replacing, GeoNet --- with cross-agency event-identifier mapping and transparent merge/conflation.
\item Apply a transparent, configurable data-quality grading scheme and report uncertainties universally, including qualitative confidence classes for historical events.
\item Stand up formal governance (a Steering Committee and a Data Stewardship Team) that operationalises both FAIR and CARE principles, underpinned by sustained multi-year funding.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Introduction}
Earthquake catalogs are the cornerstone of seismology, supporting fundamental research, operational monitoring, and risk management. In Aotearoa New Zealand, many catalogs exist, but they are fragmented, inconsistently documented, and not always interoperable. These limitations hinder both research and policy. Ensuring that earthquake catalog data are \emph{Findable, Accessible, Interoperable, and Reusable} is critical to maximise their value in science and practice. Our goal is a nationally coordinated framework and portal that makes catalog data transparent, comparable, and citable.

New Zealand's national operational catalog curated by GeoNet provides broad coverage and public access, including web and programmatic services \citep{GeoNetEqCatalogue, GeoNetFDSN, GeoNetAPI}. Internationally, initiatives such as the European Plate Observing System (EPOS) Seismology service demonstrate the benefits of standardised FAIR data services and coordinated infrastructure \citep{EPOSSeisTCS, EPOSSeisDataServices}. Importantly, the framework proposed here is not intended to replace or duplicate GeoNet's operational mandate; rather, it adds an aggregation and curation layer --- a ``catalog of catalogs'' --- that makes GeoNet's national catalog interoperable with the many regional, temporary, historical, and derived catalogs that currently sit outside any common system. This paper synthesises outcomes from two national workshops and intersession working group activity to propose a FAIR framework tailored to the New Zealand context.

\section{Current Catalog Landscape}
New Zealand's earthquake data ecosystem comprises a set of catalogs that serve different purposes. Understanding their strengths and gaps is a prerequisite to a FAIR framework.

\subsection{National Operational Catalog}
GeoNet maintains the national operational catalog and associated services, exposing event information to the public and researchers through search tools and standard interfaces \citep{GeoNetEqCatalogue, GeoNetFDSN}. Strengths include consistent coverage and accessibility throughout the country. Gaps include evolving metadata conventions across time, mixed-magnitude types, and limited exposure of some detailed processing fields to end users.

\subsection{Regional and Volcano-specific Catalogs}
Research catalogs for specific regions or processes add high-resolution detail. For example, a consistent and high-precision catalog for the Taup{\={o}} Volcanic Zone demonstrates enhanced detection and relocation accuracy in a volcanically active area \citep{Illsley2025}. These catalogs often use dense local networks or specialised methods. Their findability and interoperability can be limited if formats, identifiers, or metadata differ from national standards.

\subsection{Temporary Deployments and Experiment Catalogs}
Following significant earthquakes or for targeted studies, temporary arrays and ocean bottom deployments generate short-term catalogs with lower magnitude thresholds. These datasets are valuable, but are not routinely discoverable or merged with the national catalog without additional curation and metadata alignment.

\subsection{Historical and Derived Catalogs}
Historical catalogs compiled from written and oral records and derived catalogs for hazard modelling or research snapshots complement the instrument era. Without formal versioning, identifiers, and processing documentation, these sets can diverge from operational catalogs and be difficult to reuse or compare \citep{NSHM2022BSSA}.

\subsection*{Summary of Strengths and Gaps}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Strengths}: depth precision and completeness in local studies; consistency and access in national datasets.
\item \textbf{Gaps}: variable completeness through time and space; inconsistent processing; uncertain magnitude and depth scales; missing metadata; opaque methods; uneven access to legacy and temporary data.
\end{itemize}

\section{Earthquake Catalogs: New Zealand and International Practices}
Earthquake catalogs record key details such as time, location, depth, and magnitude, and are essential for seismic hazard analysis, research, and engineering design \citep{woessner2010instrumental, corssa_woessner}. New Zealand's catalog, maintained through GeoNet by Earth Sciences New Zealand (ESNZ; formerly GNS Science), has evolved since the 1930s, combining instrumental data and historical records \citep{GeoNetEqCatalogue, GeoNetAPI, gns_neid}. Similar catalogs exist globally (e.g., the United States Geological Survey, the Swiss Seismological Service, and the European-Mediterranean Seismological Centre). This section compares New Zealand's catalog with international counterparts, identifies core attributes of robust catalogs, and outlines steps toward a harmonised global framework for earthquake science and engineering.

\subsection{New Zealand (GeoNet)}
GeoNet maintains New Zealand's earthquake catalog as part of the National Earthquake Information Database, serving as the central repository for NZ seismic events~\citep{gns_neid}. The catalog contains origin time, location, magnitude, and seismic phase data~\citep{GeoNetEqCatalogue}, with each event assigned a unique Public ID~\citep{GeoNetEqCatalogue}. Coverage spans instrumentally recorded earthquakes (1930s--present) and historical events from oral/written records~\citep{gns_neid}.

Data access includes Quake Search (CSV, GeoJSON, KML, GML)~\citep{GeoNetEqCatalogue} and services compliant with the International Federation of Digital Seismograph Networks (FDSN) and Web Feature Service (WFS) standards, delivering QuakeML with uncertainties, phase arrivals, and multiple magnitude determinations~\citep{GeoNetFDSN,GeoNetLocationInfo}. The catalog covers NZ and offshore regions, with magnitude of completeness of approximately $M_L$ 1--2 in well-monitored regions (higher elsewhere and through time); location quality and completeness vary markedly with station coverage across the country, a heterogeneity quantified for the GeoNet catalog by \citet{WarrenSmith2025}. The catalog features automatic and reviewed solutions~\citep{GeoNetLocationInfo} and event-type classification~\citep{GeoNetFDSN}. A 2022 revision moved the catalog toward more consistent magnitude reporting nationwide~\citep{gns_neid}.

\subsection{United States (USGS ComCat)}
The United States Geological Survey (USGS) operates the federated Advanced National Seismic System (ANSS) Comprehensive Earthquake Catalog (ComCat), covering global earthquakes ($M$4.5+ complete globally; $M$1--2.5 in the US)~\citep{usgs_comcat}. It is accessed via the FDSN Event API~\citep{USGSFDSN} with QuakeML default output and optional CSV/GeoJSON/KML, plus real-time GeoJSON feeds~\citep{USGSFDSN}. Catalog entries include standard parameters plus quality metrics (stations used, azimuthal gap, RMS residuals) and magnitude uncertainty~\citep{usgs_comcat}.

ComCat integrates 100+ networks (California, Alaska, Hawaii, ISC-GEM, others), storing multiple solutions per event with one flagged as preferred~\citep{usgs_comcat}. Additional products include ShakeMap and crowdsourced intensity data~\citep{usgs_comcat}.

\subsection{Switzerland (SED)}
The Swiss Seismological Service (SED) maintains a high-quality catalog dating to 1879~\citep{sed_report}, with historical events to 250 AD. A dense network achieves $M_c$ 1.0--1.5 completeness. SED introduced updated magnitude scales ($M_{Lhc}$, 2020), employs 3D velocity models and relative relocation, and provides formal uncertainties via QuakeML since 2013~\citep{sed_report}.

Data are accessible through FDSN web services. Notable cross-border collaboration integrates 60+ stations from France, Germany, Italy, and Austria for improved border coverage~\citep{sed_report}. The ECOS project unified historical records into a homogenised database~\citep{ECOS09}.

\subsection{European/International}
The \textbf{European-Mediterranean Seismological Centre (EMSC)} operates the European Seismic Portal, aggregating data from multiple networks via FDSN-compliant services and providing multiple solutions and phase data~\citep{emsc_home,emsc_webservices}.

The \textbf{International Seismological Centre (ISC)} archives global bulletin data from 130+ agencies, accepting multiple formats (Nordic, GSE, ISF, QuakeML)~\citep{isc_data_collection} with output in ISF or QuakeML~\citep{isc_bulletin}.

Other agencies (the Japan Meteorological Agency, Geoscience Australia, and the European Integrated Data Archive) have adopted FDSN services, enabling uniform access. Major catalogs (USGS, GeoNet, SED, EMSC, ISC) now share common standards for open, interoperable data access~\citep{EPOSSeisTCS,QuakeML}.




\subsection{Gap Analysis: New Zealand vs International Best Practice}

Table~\ref{tab:catalog_comparison} provides a comprehensive comparison of New Zealand's earthquake catalog capabilities against international benchmarks, specifically the USGS ComCat (United States) and Swiss SED/EPOS systems. The comparison evaluates ten critical dimensions: core event parameters, metadata and provenance documentation, versioning and review processes, data access mechanisms, historical data integration, uncertainty quantification, multi-catalog federation, special-purpose products, standards compliance, and governance structures. While New Zealand's GeoNet catalog demonstrates strong technical capabilities, particularly in API provision and QuakeML support, several gaps emerge relative to international practice. Key priorities include: (1) universal uncertainty reporting across all catalog entries, (2) formalised version control with Digital Object Identifiers (DOIs) and changelogs, (3) development of an event-identifier mapping service to link disparate catalogs, (4) stronger integration of historical and pre-instrumental data, and (5) establishment of formal governance structures with dedicated funding. Addressing these gaps would bring New Zealand's catalog practices into line with international FAIR data leaders.

International earthquake catalogs demonstrate varying scales and capabilities. GeoNet (NZ) provides regional coverage ($M_L$ 1--2 completeness in well-monitored regions, 1930s+; with spatially variable location quality, \citealp{WarrenSmith2025}) with unique Public IDs and QuakeML support \citep{GeoNetEqCatalogue, gns_neid}. USGS ComCat operates globally (M4.5+ complete worldwide, M1--2.5 in US, 1900+), federating 100+ networks with ShakeMap products \citep{usgs_comcat}. The Swiss SED achieves exceptional resolution ($M_c$ 1.0--1.5, 1879+ with historical to 250 AD) through 3D models and cross-border collaboration \citep{sed_report}. EMSC rapidly aggregates European data with EventID mapping \citep{emsc_webservices}, while ISC archives global data from 130+ agencies as a long-term reference (1900+) \citep{isc_data_collection}.

\section{Workshop Process and Stakeholder Engagement}

\subsection{Community Workshops}
The development of the framework was community-driven. Two pre-conference workshops at the Geoscience Society of New Zealand annual meetings provided the primary engagement: a 2023 workshop framed needs and objectives, and a 2024 workshop reviewed progress and refined priorities \citep{GSNZWorkshop2023, GSNZWorkshop2024}. Participants spanned university researchers, GeoNet and Earth Sciences New Zealand staff, government agencies, and engineering and insurance users. Discussion was structured around eight thematic tables --- covering minimum catalog attributes, metadata and documentation, access and versioning, governance, and community priorities --- whose outcomes are summarised in Appendix~A. Between workshops, a working group met regularly to draft standards, test integration pathways, and design a prototype portal.

\subsection{Prototype Portal: Capabilities and Demonstration}
\label{sec:prototype}

To ground community feedback in a working system, the working group developed a functional prototype portal implementing the core workflows proposed in this framework. The prototype serves as a reference implementation that demonstrates the feasibility of the framework specifications and surfaces practical design issues early; it was used at the 2024 workshop to demonstrate ingestion, merge, export, and analytics workflows with representative New Zealand datasets. We describe it here as a proof of concept rather than a validated production service: the capabilities listed in this subsection are implemented in the prototype, whereas the national framework described in Section~\ref{sec:framework} is a proposal that, except where explicitly noted, is not yet implemented.

\textbf{Data ingestion and format support.}
The prototype accepts earthquake catalog data in multiple standard formats: CSV and tab-delimited text files with automatic delimiter and date-format detection, JSON and GeoJSON in several common structural variants, and QuakeML 1.2. A field-mapping interface allows contributors to align source column names to the canonical schema, with alias-based auto-detection to reduce manual effort. Streaming parsers handle large files without excessive memory consumption. Direct integration with GeoNet's FDSN Event Web Service supports incremental import with automatic duplicate detection.

\textbf{Search, filtering, and visualisation.}
Users can search, filter, and visualise catalogs through an interactive portal. Spatial queries using bounding boxes, drawn polygons, and radius circles are supported alongside time-range, magnitude-range, and depth filtering. An interactive map provides event clustering, uncertainty-ellipse overlays, focal mechanism visualisation (beach ball diagrams), and station coverage display. Tabular views support pagination and virtual rendering for large event sets.

\textbf{Catalog merge and conflation.}
A catalog merge engine allows users to combine multiple catalogs with configurable strategies: priority-weighted (nominated agency preference), uncertainty-weighted average, quality-based (retaining the higher-quality solution), or complete-event selection (retaining the solution with the most populated fields). The engine detects conflicts based on spatial separation, time offset, magnitude difference, depth range, and network inconsistency. Conflict logs are generated for quality control review and the resulting merged event retains lineage links to its source events. A preview mode allows users to inspect merge results before committing, including a summary of conflicts and quality-control flags.

\textbf{Analytical tools.}
Implemented capabilities include Gutenberg-Richter $b$-value estimation, completeness magnitude ($M_c$) detection, energy release analysis, cluster detection, and focal mechanism visualisation. Multi-catalog comparison tools support magnitude-frequency distribution ($b$-value comparison) and uncertainty visualisation across catalogs.

\textbf{Multi-format export.}
Catalogs and event subsets are exportable as CSV, GeoJSON, KML, and QuakeML 1.2 from both the portal and the API. Version-specific exports ensure that a cited dataset version can be retrieved exactly as it was at time of citation.

Community feedback from the 2024 workshop informed refinements to the metadata schema, merge configuration options, and quality grading approach described in the remainder of this paper.

\subsection{Metadata Standards for Catalogs}
A core metadata profile aligned with QuakeML and FDSN conventions captures identifiers, origin parameters with uncertainties, magnitude types with errors, detection and quality metrics, methodology descriptors, event classification, and catalog-level metadata \citep{QuakeML, GeoNetFDSN, USGSFDSN}. Required versus recommended fields are distinguished to balance utility and effort.

At the catalog level, required metadata include: a unique persistent identifier, catalog name and short description, contributing institution(s), temporal coverage, geographic coverage, magnitude range, event count, processing methodology summary, license, and contact information. Recommended metadata add: velocity model reference, location algorithm, phase picking method, magnitude scale definitions, quality flag descriptions, and links to associated publications.

At the event level, required fields are origin time, latitude, longitude, depth, and at least one magnitude value with its scale type. Recommended fields include origin time uncertainty, horizontal and vertical location uncertainties, magnitude uncertainty, station count, azimuthal gap, phase count, RMS residual, evaluation status (automatic or reviewed), and event type. \subsubsection{Cross-Catalog Event ID Mapping}

A known challenge in multi-agency seismology is that the same earthquake carries different public identifiers across agencies. For example, a New Zealand earthquake may appear as a GeoNet Public ID, a USGS event code, and an ISC event number. Without a mapping service, researchers aggregating data from multiple sources must resolve duplicates manually.

The framework includes an event identity mapping service that maintains a table of cross-agency equivalences. Events are matched using configurable spatial, temporal, and magnitude thresholds (illustratively, separation $<$\,20\,km, time offset $<$\,30\,s, magnitude difference $<$\,0.5). These values are deliberately permissive to absorb the differing location procedures used across agencies; the trade-off is that, in dense aftershock sequences, wide windows can group genuinely distinct events. Thresholds are therefore configurable per source, and ambiguous matches are flagged for expert review rather than merged automatically. The API exposes equivalence queries, allowing downstream tools to retrieve all known agency identifiers for a given event, deduplicate federated results, and trace the origin of merged solutions to their contributing source records.

Event ID mapping records include the matching agency, the matched identifier, a confidence score, and the thresholds applied. This service aligns with the approach used by EMSC \citep{emsc_webservices} and extends it to the New Zealand context, facilitating interoperability with USGS, ISC, and regional networks.

\subsection{Portal and Infrastructure for Data Discovery}

\subsubsection{Discovery and Search}
A central portal offers unified search and mapping, rich metadata views, download and API endpoints, and documentation. The architecture federates operational feeds and curated research datasets. Integration with international systems enhances global interoperability \citep{EPOSSeisTCS, EPOSSeisDataServices}.

The portal search interface supports free-text search, spatial filtering (bounding box, polygon, radius), time-range and magnitude-range filtering, depth filtering, and event-type filtering. Saved filter configurations allow users to reproduce a specific query for monitoring or repeated analysis. Cross-catalog search returns a merged, deduplicated result list ranked by relevance.

\subsubsection{Catalog Merge and Conflation}

Catalog conflation — combining events from multiple independent catalogs into a unified view — is a central operation in multi-agency seismology. The framework specifies the following merge strategies, all four of which are implemented in the prototype (Section~\ref{sec:prototype}); users select a strategy based on their scientific objective:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Priority-weighted}: a nominated agency's solution is preferred; solutions from other agencies are recorded as alternatives.
  \item \textbf{Uncertainty-weighted average}: location, time, and magnitude are computed as an inverse-variance-weighted mean across contributing solutions.
  \item \textbf{Quality-based}: the solution with the highest quality score (see Section~\ref{sec:quality}) is retained.
  \item \textbf{Complete-event}: the solution with the greatest number of populated recommended fields is retained.
\end{itemize}

Conflicts are detected and logged when contributing solutions disagree beyond configurable thresholds: spatial separation, temporal offset, magnitude difference, depth range, and network mismatch. Each merged event retains explicit lineage to its contributing source events and the merge configuration used, enabling full audit and reproducibility. A dry-run preview mode returns conflict statistics and quality flags without committing a new catalog version.

\subsubsection{Analytics}
The portal provides in-browser analytical tools to support quality assessment and comparative analysis. \subsection{Licensing and Access Conditions}

\subsubsection{License Requirements}
To support FAIR reusability, each dataset and metadata record should carry an explicit, machine-readable license and any access constraints \citep{FAIRPrinciples2016, GOFAIR}. For government-funded baseline products, a default open license aligned with New Zealand Government Open Access and Licensing guidance is recommended, with clearly documented exceptions for embargoed, sensitive, or culturally restricted data \citep{NZGOAL2022}. License metadata should be exposed through portal views and APIs to avoid ambiguity for downstream reuse.

\subsubsection{Role-Based Access Control}

Access to catalog data and portal operations is governed by a role-based model. Four roles are defined:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Administrator}: full access including user management, system configuration, and deletion of catalog records.
  \item \textbf{Editor}: can create and modify catalogs, import data, and initiate merge operations, but cannot manage users or alter system configuration.
  \item \textbf{Viewer}: can search, view, filter, and export catalogs to which they have been granted access, but cannot modify data.
  \item \textbf{Guest} (unauthenticated): can discover and download public catalogs; embargoed or restricted catalogs are not visible.
\end{itemize}

Role assignments are managed by Administrators and are recorded in an audit log. A role-upgrade request workflow allows registered users to request elevated access from an Administrator, with notifications and approval tracking. These four roles describe \emph{technical} access to the portal and are distinct from the \emph{governance} roles (contributor, reviewer, maintainer) used in the catalog contribution and stewardship process (Section~\ref{sec:contribution}); a single individual may hold both a system role and a governance role.

\subsubsection{Data-Level Access Control}
\label{sec:dataaccess}
Access control applies at the catalog level. Each catalog carries one of three access designations: \textbf{public} (accessible to all without authentication), \textbf{registered} (accessible to authenticated Viewers and above), or \textbf{restricted} (accessible only to named individuals or groups, as set by the Administrator). This mechanism supports time-limited embargoes, for example allowing a research group to work with a catalog privately until a companion paper is published.

Field-level masking may be applied where a catalog contains sensitive location information, such as event locations near culturally significant sites. In such cases, location coordinates may be spatially generalised or withheld for Guest and Viewer access while full precision is retained for authorised users, consistent with the CARE framework described in Section~\ref{sec:care}.

\subsubsection{Audit and Compliance}
All write operations (catalog creation, import, merge, deletion) are recorded with the authenticated user identity, timestamp, and operation details. Read access to restricted catalogs is similarly logged. These records support institutional accountability and can be provided to data owners on request. Where usage agreements are attached to a catalog (for example, a requirement to cite a companion publication), the agreement is presented at download time and acceptance is recorded.

\subsection{Versioning and Persistent Identifiers}

\subsubsection{Versioning Policy}
Each catalog release carries a version and timestamp, with change logs. Significant data sets receive DOIs to enable citation. Event identity is preserved through stable identifiers and cross-references, with aliasing where needed. All versions remain accessible for reproducibility.

A formal versioning policy distinguishes patch-level revisions (metadata corrections or other non-substantive edits) from major and minor revisions, which change the citable data content --- by altering event parameters or processing methods (major), or by adding new events or optional fields (minor) --- and therefore warrant a new version DOI \citep{DataCiteVersioning}. Version links should be recorded in metadata (for example, relations equivalent to ``IsNewVersionOf'' and ``IsPreviousVersionOf'') to maintain a clear lineage of record.

The framework adopts a three-level versioning scheme: \textsc{major.minor.patch}. Major version increments indicate changes that alter event parameters for existing events (e.g., full reprocessing with a new velocity model or magnitude calibration). Minor version increments indicate additions of new events or new optional fields without changing existing event solutions. Patch increments indicate metadata corrections, documentation updates, or bug fixes that do not affect event parameters. Table~\ref{tab:versioning} gives illustrative examples.

\subsubsection{DOI Assignment and Changelog}
The catalog series as a whole is identified by a single concept DOI, while each major and minor release receives its own version DOI minted through a recognised registration agency; patch releases update the existing version record without minting a new DOI. To avoid an unmanageable proliferation of identifiers where new events are added frequently, minor releases may be batched on a regular cadence (for example, scheduled quarterly snapshots) rather than minted per ingestion. DOI metadata includes catalog name, version, release date, geographic and temporal coverage, responsible institution, license, and relations to predecessor and successor versions.

A machine-readable changelog accompanies each release, recording the type of change (added, updated, fixed), a description, and summary statistics (total event count, new events, modified events). Changelogs are published alongside the catalog record and retrievable via API. Together, versioned DOIs and changelogs allow a researcher to cite the exact data state used in a study and allow reviewers to understand what changed between referenced versions.

\subsection{Documentation of Processing, Provenance, and Uncertainty}
Processing steps are documented, including detection, picking, location algorithms, velocity models, and magnitude calibration. Uncertainties for time, location, and magnitude are quantified or qualitatively flagged. The provenance, evaluation status, and links to raw waveforms and related products are included.

To strengthen reproducibility, provenance fields should follow a consistent model describing entities (input data and outputs), activities (processing steps), and agents (people, institutions, and software), with software version and execution date captured as minimum requirements \citep{PROVO}. This structure supports transparent audit trails across operational and research catalogs.

\subsubsection{Uncertainty Representation}

Uncertainty quantification is essential for scientific reusability. The framework specifies a standard representation to ensure consistency across catalogs and comparability in merged products.

\textbf{Origin location and time uncertainties} are reported as standard deviations: \texttt{time\_uncertainty} (seconds), \texttt{horizontal\_uncertainty} (kilometres), and \texttt{depth\_uncertainty} (kilometres). Where horizontal uncertainty is anisotropic, the semi-major and semi-minor axes of the 68\% confidence ellipse and its azimuth are reported. These map directly to QuakeML \texttt{OriginUncertainty} elements \citep{QuakeML}.

\textbf{Magnitude uncertainties} are reported as the standard deviation of the magnitude estimate on the stated scale. Because uncertainty is scale-dependent (local magnitude estimates carry different error profiles from moment magnitude estimates), the magnitude type code must always accompany the uncertainty value.

\textbf{Events without quantified uncertainties} are flagged with a boolean \texttt{uncertainty\_available} field set to false. For historical or pre-instrumental events where formal uncertainty estimation is not possible, a qualitative confidence class may be substituted (e.g.\ ``well-constrained'', ``poorly-constrained'', ``felt-only''). The data quality scoring described in Section~\ref{sec:quality} penalises records where uncertainty information is absent.

\textbf{Propagation through merges.} When events from multiple catalogs are merged, the combined uncertainty depends on the merge strategy. For uncertainty-weighted averaging, the combined standard deviation is computed from inverse-variance weighting. For priority or quality-based strategies, the selected solution's uncertainty is retained and the alternatives recorded. The merge log documents which strategy was applied and the resulting uncertainty for each merged event.

\subsection{Data Quality Assessment Framework}
\label{sec:quality}

\subsubsection{Quality Scoring Methodology}
A standardised quality scoring system enables consistent evaluation of catalog completeness and reliability, supporting informed reuse decisions. Quality is assessed across five dimensions:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Completeness}: fraction of required and recommended fields populated (weighted: required fields carry higher weight than recommended fields).
  \item \textbf{Accuracy}: internal consistency checks, including depth--magnitude plausibility, non-negative uncertainty values, and coordinates within the catalog's declared geographic bounds.
  \item \textbf{Reliability}: proportion of events with reviewed (versus automatic) solutions, station count and azimuthal gap relative to thresholds, and phase count adequacy.
  \item \textbf{Consistency}: temporal ordering, absence of duplicate events (by time and location), and cross-field agreement (e.g.\ magnitude type codes matching declared scale definitions).
  \item \textbf{Traceability}: availability of processing documentation, at least one contact record, and a link to the catalog version record or companion publication.
\end{enumerate}

An overall quality score (0--100) is computed as a weighted sum across dimensions (illustrative weights: 40\% completeness, 20\% accuracy, 15\% reliability, 15\% consistency, 10\% traceability). These weights, and the grade thresholds in Table~\ref{tab:quality_grades}, are an illustrative starting point rather than an empirically calibrated standard: they should be tuned by the community against representative New Zealand catalogs, and the scoring scheme is designed so that both weights and thresholds are configurable. The score maps to a letter grade as shown in Table~\ref{tab:quality_grades}.

\subsubsection{Specific Quality Checks}
Representative automated checks include: events ordered chronologically; no future-dated events; depth within a physically plausible range for the region (e.g.\ $-5$ to 1000\,km, the negative bound allowing for events located above the sea-level datum); magnitude within a plausible range for the declared region; uncertainty values non-negative and within parameter-specific sanity bounds (for example, horizontal uncertainty not exceeding the catalog's declared spatial extent, and magnitude uncertainty below a configurable ceiling such as 1.0 magnitude unit); no coordinate pair repeated for distinct events at an identical time (duplicate detection); station count $\geq$\,5 for the event to be considered well-constrained; and azimuthal gap $<$\,180° for reliable depth estimates \citep{usgs_comcat}. Results are summarised as passed, warning, or failed for each check category.

\subsubsection{Anomaly Detection}
Statistical outlier detection flags events whose parameters fall outside expected distributions for the catalog as a whole. Events with unusually high magnitudes relative to surrounding events in time and space, or with depths inconsistent with the regional tectonic setting, are flagged for expert review. Flagged events are not removed automatically; the system annotates them so that users can apply appropriate caution.

\subsection{Special Catalog Types and Integration}

Different catalog types require adapted handling to preserve scientific value while meeting FAIR standards.

\subsubsection{Regional Research Catalogs}
Regional catalogs produced with specialised methods (e.g.\ 3D velocity models, relative relocation, custom magnitude calibrations) are ingested as distinct catalogs with their own version records and DOIs. Processing methods are documented in the catalog-level metadata, including references to the velocity model and location algorithm used. Regional catalogs are not automatically merged into the national operational catalog because different processing assumptions may introduce systematic biases; instead, the portal provides cross-reference links between co-temporal events in regional and national catalogs, with the spatial and magnitude offset reported so that researchers can assess consistency.

\subsubsection{Temporary Deployment Catalogs}
Catalogs from aftershock deployments or targeted experiments are ingested with an explicit time window and a designation of ``research'' or ``monitoring'' type. Station geometry metadata (array bounds or individual station codes) is stored as catalog-level attributes. Where waveform data are archived, links to the relevant FDSN waveform service are included. Temporary deployment catalogs are searchable and downloadable alongside permanent catalogs but are labelled to indicate their limited geographic and temporal scope.

\subsubsection{Historical and Pre-Instrumental Catalogs}
Historical catalogs present distinct challenges: location precision may be tens of kilometres, magnitude estimates are converted from macroseismic intensity or historical accounts, and formal uncertainty estimation is often not possible. The framework accommodates these catalogs through:

\begin{itemize}
  \item Qualitative location confidence classes (e.g.\ ``well-constrained'', ``approximate'', ``felt-only'') in place of or alongside numeric uncertainties.
  \item Support for macroseismic intensity fields (Modified Mercalli or EMS-98 \citep{Grunthal1998}) alongside or in place of instrumental magnitude.
  \item Narrative description fields for source attribution (e.g.\ newspaper records, missionary accounts, oral tradition references).
  \item Separate quality thresholds for historical records, reflecting their inherently lower parameter precision.
\end{itemize}

Historical catalogs are not merged with instrumental catalogs by default; they are presented as a separate collection with explicit data type labelling. Cross-reference links to instrumentally recorded aftershocks or re-analyses may be added where they exist.

\subsubsection{Derived and Synthetic Catalogs}
Derived catalogs (filtered subsets, hazard snapshots, or synthetic seismicity forecasts) are ingested with mandatory provenance metadata identifying the source catalog(s), derivation method, parameters, and software. Derived catalog records carry explicit ``derivedFrom'' relations to their source catalog versions, enabling users to trace a derived product back to the underlying data. Synthetic catalogs used in probabilistic seismic hazard analysis are labelled as ``synthetic'' to distinguish them from observational records.

\subsection{Aotearoa New Zealand Data Governance Context}
\label{sec:care}
In Aotearoa New Zealand, the framework should be implemented in a way that is consistent with both FAIR and CARE principles, so that data stewardship recognises Indigenous rights, authority, and benefit alongside openness \citep{CAREPrinciples2020}. For catalogs incorporating mātauranga-informed interpretations, iwi-relevant records, or sensitive contextual information, governance processes should document appropriate access conditions, attribution, and engagement pathways.

Where event records relate to locations of cultural significance or incorporate traditional ecological knowledge, the framework's data-level access control (Section~\ref{sec:dataaccess}) provides one technical mechanism to restrict or mask sensitive fields. This is, however, only a mechanism: the appropriate access conditions for such records must be determined with, not for, iwi and hapū, who should be engaged as partners and decision-makers from the outset rather than consulted as data subjects after the design is fixed. The Steering Committee's membership (Section~\ref{sec:steering}) should include representation from relevant Māori communities and data sovereignty advocates to ensure that CARE principles are operationalised rather than aspirational.

\section{Implementation Recommendations}

\subsection{Governance, Roles, and Responsibilities}

\subsubsection{Steering Committee}
\label{sec:steering}
A Steering Committee provides strategic oversight and ensures community ownership of the framework. Membership should include representatives from Earth Sciences New Zealand, at least two universities with active seismology programmes, a government user (e.g.\ National Emergency Management Agency), an engineering or insurance sector representative, an Indigenous data sovereignty advocate, and an international advisor from a peer organisation (e.g.\ ISC, EMSC, or GFZ). The Committee meets at minimum quarterly, with defined decision-making processes and staggered three-year terms to ensure continuity.

The Committee is responsible for approving new data sources and catalogs for ingestion, ratifying versioning decisions that affect the national record, overseeing international partnerships and alignment with EPOS or similar consortia, and commissioning annual reporting against FAIR compliance indicators.

\subsubsection{Data Stewardship Team}
\label{sec:steward}
A Data Stewardship Team led by Earth Sciences New Zealand handles day-to-day operations. Indicative resourcing for a mature service includes: a Data Stewardship Lead (oversight, Steering Committee liaison), Data Managers (catalog ingestion, metadata curation, quality review), a Software Engineer (API maintenance, infrastructure, security), and a User Support Analyst (community queries, documentation, training). Budget should cover staffing, infrastructure (compute, storage, backup), and biennial framework reviews.

\subsubsection{Catalog Contribution Process}
\label{sec:contribution}
External researchers and institutions may submit catalogs for inclusion. Submissions require: catalog name and description, responsible institution and contact, time period and geographic coverage, event count and magnitude range, format and metadata documentation, and a declared license. A two-stage review process applies: technical QC (format validity, completeness score, duplicate check) followed by scientific peer review (methods assessment, comparison with existing catalogs). The expected review timeline is four to eight weeks, with written feedback provided regardless of outcome. Approved catalogs are assigned a DOI and version record.

\subsubsection{Success Metrics and Annual Reporting}
Progress should be measured and reported annually against indicators including: number of catalogs ingested; total event count and geographic coverage; proportion of catalogs with quality grade A or above; API uptime (target: 99.5\%); registered user count; number of data downloads and API queries; and number of peer-reviewed publications citing the platform or a versioned catalog DOI. Reporting against FAIR sub-principle indicators (Section~\ref{sec:faircompliance}) should also be included. Annual reports should be published openly to maintain transparency and accountability.

Illustrative two-year targets: the full GeoNet national catalog (well over one million events) plus 20 or more additional regional, temporary, and historical catalogs accessible through a single interface; 200 or more registered users drawn from the New Zealand and international research, engineering, and agency communities; 10 or more research outputs citing a versioned catalog DOI; and API availability exceeding 99\% (building toward the 99.5\% operational target). These figures are indicative planning targets, not commitments, and should be revisited once the service is established.

\subsection{Timeline for Development and Adoption}
\textbf{Phase 1: Planning and mobilisation (0--6 months)}. Formalise governance and resources. Finalise the metadata schema and technical architecture. Establish the Steering Committee and Data Stewardship Team.\\
\textbf{Phase 2: Prototyping and initial integration (6--18 months)}. Build core portal and APIs. Integrate pilot catalogs (GeoNet national catalog plus 5--10 regional and temporary datasets). Refine standards based on contributor feedback. Deliver community training workshops.\\
\textbf{Phase 3: Full-scale implementation (18--36 months)}. Ingest major regional and temporary datasets. Harden and launch the public portal. Activate event ID mapping service. Establish DOI minting workflow.\\
\textbf{Phase 4: Maintenance and improvement (36+ months)}. Operate as a national core service with annual review, FAIR compliance reporting, and planned capability enhancements informed by community feedback.

\subsection{Funding and Support Considerations}
Secure multi-year funding through research infrastructure programmes and operational budgets. Budget for data curation and development roles, storage and delivery costs, and user support. Indicative resourcing for a mature service is on the order of three to four full-time-equivalent (FTE) staff --- a stewardship lead, data managers, a software engineer, and part-time user support (Section~\ref{sec:steward}) --- together with cloud or on-premises infrastructure for storage, compute, and backup, and recurring DOI registration fees. A realistic path begins with a smaller team during Phases 1--2 and scales to the full complement at Phase 3. The figures here are indicative of scale rather than costed estimates; precise budgets should be developed with host institutions. Measure success with adoption metrics, integrated catalog counts, and demonstrable impact on science and hazard modelling.

\section{Benefits}
\textbf{Reproducibility}. Versioned datasets with persistent identifiers and clear processing history enable verification and reuse.\\
\textbf{Science and innovation}. Integrated and standardised data reduce barriers and accelerate discovery, aligned with international best practice \citep{EPOSSeisTCS}.\\
\textbf{Risk and hazard}. Trusted, timely, and comparable inputs improve hazard and risk assessments and operational products \citep{NSHM2022BSSA}.\\
\textbf{Collaboration and efficiency}. Shared rules, tools, and data reduce duplication and strengthen national capabilities.

\section{Conclusion and Call to Action}
New Zealand has the data, the expertise, and a clear community mandate to make its earthquake catalogs FAIR; what is missing is a coordinating framework and the governance to sustain it. This white paper has set out that framework, benchmarked it against international practice, and demonstrated its feasibility through a working prototype. The remaining step is collective commitment.

We invite GeoNet and Earth Sciences New Zealand, universities, government agencies, iwi and hapū partners, and the engineering and insurance sectors to:
\begin{enumerate}
\setlength{\itemsep}{1pt}
\item endorse the metadata, lexicon, versioning, and quality standards proposed here as a shared national baseline;
\item commit to establishing the Steering Committee and Data Stewardship Team (Section~\ref{sec:steering}), with representation that operationalises both FAIR and CARE; and
\item support a multi-year funding case to move from prototype to a sustained national service.
\end{enumerate}
We welcome feedback on this draft and expressions of interest in participating in the governance and catalog-contribution processes described above. Comments may be directed to the corresponding author.

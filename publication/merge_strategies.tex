\section{Strategies for Merging Earthquake \Catalogs: A Literature Review}
\label{sec:merging}

% --  overview paragraph  -----------------------------------------------------

The construction of unified earthquake \catalogs\ from multiple heterogeneous
source networks is one of the central methodological challenges of observational
seismology.  The need arises at every level of the discipline: global hazard
models require decades of stable, magnitude-homogeneous records spanning
multiple agencies \citep{Pagani2014}; regional seismic-rate studies must combine
specialist national networks with global bulletins to fill spatial coverage gaps
\citep{Woessner2005}; historical and pre-instrumental periods demand careful
integration of macroseismic and early-instrumental data
\citep{Grunthal1998, CPTI15}; and the growth of dense, heterogeneous strong-
motion and nodal-sensor networks creates new challenges at sub-local scales
\citep{Cochran2020}.

Despite the ubiquity of the problem, no single standard algorithmic pipeline
has emerged across the community.  Different groups have developed independent
solutions—from the rule-based proximity approaches of the
\citeauthor{NCEDCComCat}~(\citeyear{NCEDCComCat}) and the ISC bulletin
\citep{Bondár2011}, to the graph-theoretic frameworks of
\citet{Benz2019}, to the physics-based nearest-neighbour discriminant of
\citet{Tanaka2022}—each optimised for different data volumes, network
configurations, and seismicity regimes.  The goal of this review is to
synthesise the methodological state of the art into a coherent framework that
can inform automated merging implementations.

\subsection{The Two-Step Decomposition}
\label{sec:twostep}

Any robust merging pipeline must separate two operations that are logically
distinct but are frequently conflated in practice:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Event association and deduplication} — determining which records
        across source \catalogs\ describe the \emph{same physical earthquake}
        and grouping them into equivalence classes.

  \item \textbf{Parameter selection and homogenisation} — from each equivalence
        class, deriving a single best origin time, hypocentre, and magnitude,
        and expressing all magnitudes on a common scale.
\end{enumerate}

The distinction matters because applying a magnitude conversion formula
\emph{before} the association step—for instance to make thresholds
magnitude-scale-independent—introduces feedback loops: converted magnitudes may
shift an event across an association threshold, altering which pairs are grouped,
which in turn changes the sample from which conversion coefficients are estimated
\citep{Storchak2013, Grunthal2016}.  The correct order is always: associate
first using raw reported values; homogenise second using the confirmed groups.

\subsection{Historical Development and the Heterogeneous Network Problem}
\label{sec:history}

\subsubsection{The pre-digital era: 1900–1970}

Early global earthquake \catalogs—the Gutenberg-Richter Seismicity of the Earth
\citep{GutenbergRichter1954}, the Abe \catalog\ of large historical earthquakes
\citep{Abe1981}, and the pre-1964 portion of the Engdahl-van der Hilst-Buland
(EHB) dataset \citep{Engdahl1998}—were assembled by manual examination of
seismogram archives, station bulletins, and published macroseismic accounts.
Cross-network event identification was performed by inspection, using timing
differences of several minutes and epicentral discrepancies of hundreds of
kilometres as acceptable tolerances for the analogue recording era
\citep{Bondár2011}.

The International Seismological Summary (ISS, predecessor to the ISC bulletin)
began assembling global phase data in 1918.  Its practice of retaining all
contributing agency solutions alongside a centralised re-location—still followed
by the ISC today—established the principle of \emph{solution provenance} that
underpins modern best practice \citep{ISCBulletinRebuild2020}.

\subsubsection{The WWSSN and NEIC era: 1960–1990}

The deployment of the World-Wide Standardized Seismograph Network (WWSSN) from
1961 onward provided the first globally uniform recording infrastructure and
enabled systematic relocation of events at $M \geq 5$ using common velocity
models \citep{Engdahl1998}.  The 1964 establishment of the National Earthquake
Information Center (NEIC, initially the National Earthquake Information Service)
created the first dedicated fusion centre for global earthquake data, introducing
the concept of a \emph{preferred solution} from a ranked list of contributing
agencies \citep{usgs_comcat}.

The ANSS Composite \catalog, which consolidates the NEIC global bulletin with
authoritative regional networks across North America, employs a proximity rule
of 16\,s in time and 100\,km in distance to identify duplicate reports submitted
by different networks for the same event \citep{NCEDCComCat}.  This rule was
formulated for the digital WWSSN era and remains the most precisely documented
operational threshold in any publicly available system documentation.

\subsubsection{The digital broadband era: 1990–present}

The transition to digital broadband recording in the late 1980s and 1990s, the
expansion of the IRIS/IDA Global Seismographic Network (GSN), and the growth of
the FDSN data-exchange standard \citep{QuakeML} transformed the scale of the
deduplication problem.  By 2010, the ISC bulletin was receiving origin reports
from more than 130 contributing agencies worldwide, each applying different
velocity models, phase identification algorithms, and location programs
\citep{ISCBulletinRebuild2020}.  The ISC-GEM Global Instrumental Earthquake
\Catalog\ \citep{Storchak2013, ISCGEM2018}, released in 2013 and subsequently
extended through 2021, represents the most comprehensive attempt to
retrospectively homogenise this heterogeneous archive for events $M \geq 5.5$.

The proliferation of dense regional networks—GeoNet in Aotearoa New Zealand
\citep{GeoNetEqCatalogue}, Hi-net in Japan \citep{Obara2005}, the Southern
California Seismic Network \citep{Hutton2010}, and the European EPOS infrastructure
\citep{EPOSSeisTCS}—has simultaneously improved detection thresholds for small
events ($M < 3$) while creating new challenges for merging: specialist local
networks now routinely detect thousands of microseismic events per year that
global agencies never report, making one-to-one association across scales
difficult \citep{Woessner2005, Illsley2025}.

\subsection{Event Association: Spatio-Temporal Matching Algorithms}
\label{sec:matching}

\subsubsection{Physical basis for proximity thresholds}
\label{sec:physicalbasis}

The rationale for any spatio-temporal matching window is that the reported
origin time and hypocentre of two records for the same physical earthquake will
differ only by the combined effect of (i) timing errors at each recording
network, (ii) location uncertainties arising from network geometry and velocity
model errors, and (iii) any deliberate choices such as depth fixing.

For digital networks with GPS-synchronised clocks, origin time errors are
typically well below 1\,s \citep{Bondár2011}.  Location errors depend strongly
on network geometry: the azimuthal gap (the largest angular gap between
contributing stations) and the minimum station distance are the dominant
predictors.  \citet{Bondár2004} showed empirically from the ISC bulletin that
the 90th percentile of location error is approximately:

\begin{equation}
  \sigma_r \approx 0.51 + 0.038\,\text{GAP} + 0.0055\,\Delta_{\min}^{-1},
  \label{eq:locerrBondar}
\end{equation}

where GAP is the azimuthal gap in degrees and $\Delta_{\min}$ is the closest
station distance in degrees.  For a typical global network with
GAP $\approx 120^\circ$ and $\Delta_{\min} \approx 3^\circ$, this yields
$\sigma_r \approx 5$\,km.  For poorly-constrained events with
GAP $\approx 270^\circ$, $\sigma_r$ can exceed 50\,km, justifying wider
matching windows.

Pre-digital era timing errors of several seconds per station translate to origin
time uncertainties of 1--10\,s for well-recorded events and up to 30--60\,s for
poorly-recorded events in the pre-1960 period \citep{Bondár2011}.  This is the
physical basis for the wider windows in \cref{tab:windows}.

\subsubsection{Window-based forward-sweep}
\label{sec:forwardsweep}

The oldest and most widely deployed method sorts all incoming origin reports by
origin time and scans forward with a sliding window.  If two reports from
\emph{different} networks fall within a fixed time window $\Delta t_{\max}$ and
an epicentral distance $\Delta d_{\max}$, they are flagged as candidate
duplicates \citep{NCEDCComCat, Woessner2005}.  \Cref{tab:windows} summarises
documented threshold choices from major operational systems.

The 16\,s / 100\,km rule of the ANSS is the most precisely documented publicly
available threshold for a production system.  It was chosen empirically to
balance false-positive (erroneously merging distinct earthquakes) against
false-negative (missing genuine duplicate pairs) rates for the population of
events in the North American digital-era database \citep{NCEDCComCat}.  For
teleseismic events the 60\,s / 100\,km window is widely cited; the ISC uses a
comparable criterion internally when auto-grouping agency solutions prior to
relocation \citep{ISCBulletinRebuild2020}.

\paragraph{Algorithmic complexity and spatial indexing.}
A naive implementation of the forward sweep compares all ordered pairs
$(E_i, E_j)$ with $j > i$, giving $O(n^2)$ complexity for $n$ events.
For large \catalogs\ ($n > 10^5$), this is prohibitive.  The standard
optimisation builds a \emph{spatial index} over event locations: a uniform
grid (hash grid), k-d tree, or R-tree, allowing events within $\Delta d_{\max}$
to be retrieved in $O(\log n)$ time.  Combined with a time-sorted event list
and an early-exit rule when the candidate's origin time exceeds
$t_i + \Delta t_{\max}$, the algorithm achieves $O(n \log n)$ overall
complexity in the typical case \citep{Benz2019}.

Grid-based indexes use a fixed cell size calibrated to the distance threshold.
At high latitudes, degrees of longitude subtend shorter distances, so the cell
size must be adjusted by $\cos(\phi)$ where $\phi$ is the latitude
\citep{Tan2021}.  Near the International Date Line (longitude $\pm 180^\circ$),
grid cell keys must wrap correctly; failure to implement wrapping silently loses
candidates for events in the Pacific \citep{DeadSea2022}.

\paragraph{Adaptive thresholds.}
Fixed windows perform poorly across the full magnitude range because larger
events are subject to greater timing and location uncertainty at distant
networks.  The standard remedy, used in the ISC-GEM pipeline, is to scale the
effective window by event magnitude and depth \citep{ISCGEM2018}:

\begin{equation}
  \Delta t_{\mathrm{eff}} = \Delta t_{\mathrm{config}} \cdot f_t(M),
  \qquad
  \Delta d_{\mathrm{eff}} = \Delta d_{\mathrm{config}} \cdot f_d(M) \cdot g(h),
  \label{eq:adaptive}
\end{equation}

where representative multiplier functions consistent with ISC-GEM practice
\citep{ISCGEM2018, Engdahl2020} are:

\begin{align}
  f_t(M) &=
  \begin{cases}
    1.0 & M < 4.0 \\
    1.5 & 4.0 \leq M < 5.5 \\
    2.0 & 5.5 \leq M < 7.0 \\
    3.0 & M \geq 7.0
  \end{cases}
  \label{eq:ft}
  \\[6pt]
  f_d(M) &=
  \begin{cases}
    1.0 & M < 4.0 \\
    1.5 & 4.0 \leq M < 5.5 \\
    2.5 & 5.5 \leq M < 7.0 \\
    4.0 & M \geq 7.0
  \end{cases}
  \label{eq:fd}
  \\[6pt]
  g(h) &=
  \begin{cases}
    1.0 & h < 100\,\text{km} \\
    1.2 & 100 \leq h < 300\,\text{km} \\
    1.5 & h \geq 300\,\text{km}
  \end{cases}
  \label{eq:gh}
\end{align}

The depth multiplier $g(h)$ reflects the well-established result that intermediate
and deep-focus earthquakes are harder to locate precisely because depth-sensitive
phases (pP, sP) are often absent from or misidentified in network phase datasets
\citep{Engdahl1998, Engdahl2020}.  For $M \geq 7$ deep events, the combined
multiplier can reach $4.0 \times 1.5 = 6.0$, requiring the spatial search
radius to extend to six times the configured baseline threshold.

\paragraph{Early termination and threshold consistency.}
A subtle but important correctness issue arises when combining the forward-sweep
time-sorted early exit with adaptive thresholds.  The early-exit condition
\begin{equation}
  t_j - t_i > \Delta t_{\mathrm{config}} \cdot f_t(M_i)
  \label{eq:earlyexit}
\end{equation}
uses only the anchor event's magnitude $M_i$.  However, the adaptive matching
function \eqref{eq:adaptive} uses the \emph{average} of both event magnitudes.
If the candidate $j$ has a substantially higher magnitude than $i$, the correct
effective threshold (evaluated at the average magnitude) may exceed the
early-exit threshold (evaluated at $M_i$ alone), causing valid matches to be
discarded.  The conservative fix is to replace $f_t(M_i)$ in
\eqref{eq:earlyexit} with $f_t(\max(M_i, M_j))$, or equivalently to use the
maximum expected candidate magnitude (e.g.\ $M_i + 2$) as a conservative upper
bound when evaluating the exit condition \citep{Benz2019}.

\paragraph{Limitation: dense aftershock sequences.}
During intense aftershock sequences the inter-event time between consecutive
legitimate aftershocks can fall well below the duplicate-detection window.
\citet{Tanaka2022} demonstrated that in the 24 hours following the 2011 Tōhoku
$M_\mathrm{w}$\,9.0, more than 599 genuine aftershock pairs had inter-event
times under 60\,s — the same interval used as a duplicate window in the ISC-GEM
pipeline.  Fixed-window methods therefore cannot discriminate a duplicate pair
from an aftershock pair in high-seismicity periods without additional physical
information, a problem that grows more severe as network density increases and
detection completeness lowers the magnitude threshold into the range where
aftershock rates are high \citep{Hainzl2016}.

\subsubsection{Nearest-neighbour proximity function}
\label{sec:nn}

\citet{Tanaka2022} introduced a physics-based proximity function that
discriminates duplicate pairs from aftershock pairs by exploiting their
different topological signatures in event-pair space.  The method builds on the
nearest-neighbour statistics introduced by \citet{Zaliapin2008} for aftershock
sequence identification.  Genuine duplicates form \emph{isolated pairs} in
rescaled event space — their nearest-neighbour distances cluster tightly below
a network-error-derived threshold — whereas aftershocks form \emph{branching
trees} following Omori-law temporal decay \citep{Omori1894, Utsu1961}.

The proximity between two events $i$ and $j$ (with $j$ following $i$ in time)
is defined as

\begin{equation}
  \eta(i,j) = t_{ij}\; r_{ij}^{d_f}\; 10^{-b m_i},
  \label{eq:proximity}
\end{equation}

where $t_{ij}$ is the inter-event time (s), $r_{ij}$ is the epicentral distance
(km), $d_f \approx 1.6$ is the fractal dimension of the epicentre distribution
\citep{Kagan1991}, $b \approx 1.0$ is the Gutenberg-Richter $b$-value
\citep{Aki1965}, and $m_i$ is the magnitude of the preceding event.  In this
rescaled space, aftershocks cluster near their parent event (small $\eta$),
while duplicates cluster near zero regardless of the parent-event magnitude
\citep{Zaliapin2008, Tanaka2022}.

The duplicate-detection threshold in $\eta$-space is calibrated against the
distribution of location errors for the specific network under analysis, not
against earthquake physics.  This is the key advantage: the threshold adapts
to network capability rather than assuming a fixed proximity tolerance
\citep{Tanaka2022}.  Applied to the Tōhoku sequence, the method achieved
duplicate identification reliability $> 97$\,\% even during periods with
aftershock rates of several hundred events per hour.

\paragraph{Parameter estimation.}
The fractal dimension $d_f$ can be estimated from the slope of the correlation
integral of hypocentre coordinates in log-log space \citep{Kagan1991}.  The
$b$-value is estimated via maximum likelihood from the observed
frequency-magnitude distribution \citep{Aki1965}, with magnitude of
completeness $M_c$ determined by the \citet{Wiemer2000} goodness-of-fit method
before fitting.  Both estimates should be computed from the target region's
seismicity, not borrowed from global averages, as both parameters show
significant regional variability \citep{Woessner2005}.

\paragraph{Limitations.}
The nearest-neighbour method performs less well for very sparse \catalogs\ where
the nearest-neighbour distance distribution is dominated by background seismicity
rather than aftershock clusters, reducing the bimodal separation that enables
discrimination \citep{Hainzl2016}.  It also requires a minimum \catalog\ length
(typically several years) to estimate $b$ and $d_f$ reliably.

\subsubsection{Graph-based connected-components association}
\label{sec:graph}

The methodologically strongest general-purpose approach constructs an undirected
association graph $G = (V, E)$ in which each node $v \in V$ is an origin report
and each edge $e \in E$ connects a pair of reports that satisfy a proximity
criterion \citep{Benz2019, Zaliapin2013}.  Connected components of $G$,
recoverable in $O(n\,\alpha(n))$ time via the Union-Find (Disjoint-Set Union)
algorithm \citep{Tarjan1975} — where $\alpha$ is the inverse Ackermann
function and is effectively constant for all practical $n$ — correspond to
clusters of reports describing the same physical earthquake.

\paragraph{Algorithm.}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Sort all origin reports by origin time; build a spatial index (uniform
        grid, k-d tree, or R-tree) over event hypocentres.
  \item For each report $E_i$, query the index for all reports within
        $\Delta t_{\max}$ and $\Delta d_{\max}$ from networks \emph{other than}
        the network of $E_i$.
  \item For each qualifying candidate pair $(E_i, E_j)$, add edge $e_{ij}$ to
        $G$.
  \item Find all connected components of $G$ using Union-Find.
  \item For each component, apply parameter-selection rules
        (\cref{sec:selection}) to derive a single merged event record.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Advantage over the greedy forward sweep.}
The greedy sweep implicitly assumes that pairwise matching is transitive: if
event A matches B, and B matches C, all three are merged.  In the sweep
implementation this is only guaranteed when events are processed strictly in
time order and no candidate is marked processed before its later matches are
evaluated.  When the sweep marks B as processed upon matching with A, a
subsequent evaluation of C against B is never performed, and the A-B-C cluster
is incorrectly split \citep{Benz2019}.  The graph approach has no such
pathology: transitivity is guaranteed by the connected-component structure.

\paragraph{Integer linear programming refinement.}
\citet{Benz2019} extended the graph approach by applying Integer Linear
Programming (ILP) to resolve ambiguous components — cases where a connected
component may span two closely spaced but genuinely distinct earthquakes.  The
ILP minimises the total number of source events $k$ required to explain all
edges in a component, subject to the constraint that no two reports assigned to
the same source event originate from the same network:

\begin{equation}
  \text{minimise} \quad k
  \quad \text{subject to} \quad
  \forall (i,j) \in E : \mathrm{source}(i) = \mathrm{source}(j),
  \quad
  \forall i,j : \mathrm{net}(i) = \mathrm{net}(j) \Rightarrow \mathrm{source}(i) \neq \mathrm{source}(j).
  \label{eq:ilp}
\end{equation}

Applied to the Chilean catalogue 2010--2017, this two-stage approach detected
817\,000+ earthquakes at $> 95$\,\% accuracy against a synthetic validation
dataset generated by forward modelling known network detection probabilities.

The ILP constraint that \emph{no two reports from the same network belong to the
same source event} is the algorithmic expression of what we term the
\emph{same-agency duplicate flag}: if two records from the same agency fall
within the proximity window, they almost certainly describe two distinct physical
earthquakes, not two observations of one \citep{Benz2019, Tanaka2022}.

The USGS NEIC Hydra production system uses a graph-association framework
conceptually equivalent to the first stage of \citet{Benz2019}
\citep{usgs_comcat}.

\subsection{Location Uncertainty and Its Effect on Matching}
\label{sec:locuncert}

\subsubsection{Sources of location uncertainty}

Hypocentre location uncertainty arises from five primary sources
\citep{Bondár2004, Engdahl1998}:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Phase picking errors.}  Manual or automated picks of $P$, $S$,
        and depth phases carry random timing errors (typically 0.1--1\,s for
        digital data, 1--5\,s for analogue).

  \item \textbf{Network geometry.}  The azimuthal gap and minimum station
        distance control the conditioning of the location inversion.  A gap
        $> 180^\circ$ leaves the hypocentre poorly constrained in the
        ungapped direction; a large minimum distance makes depth poorly
        constrained.

  \item \textbf{Velocity model errors.}  All location algorithms use an
        approximation to Earth structure; residual travel-time prediction errors
        are correlated among nearby stations, biasing the location estimate.
        \citet{Bondár2011} introduced a correlated error covariance model to
        account for this.

  \item \textbf{Depth-phase absence.}  Without $pP$, $sP$, or $pwP$ phases,
        depth is often fixed at a default value (10, 33, or 100\,km depending
        on agency convention), introducing a systematic depth error that can
        exceed 50\,km \citep{Engdahl1998, Engdahl2020}.

  \item \textbf{Reporting delays and partial phase sets.}  Rapid preliminary
        solutions from real-time networks (e.g.\ NEIC PDL) use fewer phases
        than the reviewed bulletin solution and therefore carry larger
        uncertainties \citep{usgs_comcat}.
\end{enumerate}

\subsubsection{Quantifying location quality for merging decisions}

The ISC bulletin quality scoring system \citep{Bondár2011} provides a practical
framework for weighting competing hypocentre solutions within a merged group.
The primary quality indicators are:

\begin{itemize}
  \item Azimuthal gap: $\text{GAP} < 180^\circ$ for acceptable, $< 120^\circ$
        for good.
  \item Secondary azimuthal gap (gap ignoring the single most distant station):
        $< 180^\circ$ is the ISC-GEM criterion for a usable depth
        \citep{ISCGEM2018}.
  \item Number of defining phases: $\geq 6$ minimum; $\geq 20$ for well-
        constrained depth.
  \item RMS travel-time residual: $< 1.0$\,s for well-located events
        \citep{Bondár2011}; the ISC bulletin flags events with
        RMS $> 2.0$\,s as poorly located \citep{ISCBulletinRebuild2020}.
  \item Horizontal uncertainty: expressed as the semi-major axis of the 90\,\%
        confidence ellipse in km \citep{Bondár2004}.
\end{itemize}

These metrics should be used to rank competing solutions within a merged
event group (\cref{sec:selection}), not merely to flag events after the
fact.

\subsubsection{Depth fixing and its consequences}

A significant fraction of agencies fix the focal depth at a standard value when
insufficient depth-sensitive phases are available.  \citet{Engdahl2020} estimate
that approximately 30\,\% of events in the pre-1990 ISC bulletin have fixed
depths.  Fixed-depth solutions carry no depth uncertainty information and should
be assigned lowest priority in any depth-selection step.  The depth type
(free or fixed, and if fixed, the value and reason) should be preserved as event
metadata to enable downstream filtering \citep{ISCGEM2018, Engdahl2020}.

The three most common fixed-depth conventions are 10\,km (shallow crustal
default, used by many regional agencies), 33\,km (Conrad discontinuity, a
historical NEIC convention), and 100\,km (subduction-zone intermediate-depth
default).  Events with these three depths should be treated with heightened
scrutiny in any depth-sensitive analysis \citep{Engdahl1998}.

\subsection{The ISC-GEM Reference Methodology}
\label{sec:iscgem}

The ISC-GEM Global Instrumental Earthquake \Catalog\
\citep{Storchak2013, ISCGEM2018} is the most thoroughly documented end-to-end
merging pipeline in the seismological literature.  Its fifth revision
\citep{ISCGEM2018} covers 1904--2014 for $M_\mathrm{w} \geq 5.5$ globally and
$M_\mathrm{w} \geq 5.0$ over continental regions, with subsequent extensions to
2021.  The pipeline is described in sufficient detail to serve as a reference
implementation for any automated merging system.

\subsubsection{Data sources and ingestion}

Source data include the ISC bulletin (phase data and agency origin solutions
from 1904 onward), the NEIC PDE and predecessors, GCMT solutions from 1976
\citep{Dziewonski1981, Ekstrom2012}, regional CMT and MT catalogues (GeoNet,
INGV, GEOFON, ETH, NIED), and macroseismic intensity data for the pre-1960
period \citep{Storchak2013}.  Each source is assigned a priority tier and a
data quality flag based on known limitations (e.g.\ analogue vs.\ digital
recording, reporting latency, known velocity model biases).

\subsubsection{Event association}

The ISC automated grouping algorithm compares origin times and epicentre
coordinates from all contributing agencies and assembles candidate groups using
a proximity-scoring system that weights azimuthal gap, phase count, distance
range, and network geometry \citep{Bondár2011}.  Groups that appear to span two
distinct earthquakes — identified by anomalously large within-group
hypocentre scatter or by the same-agency rule — are split by the ISC analyst
team \citep{ISCBulletinRebuild2020}.

\subsubsection{Relocation using all available phase data}

The ISC does not simply select the best existing agency solution; it
\emph{re-locates every event} from scratch using all contributed phase data.
The two-stage relocation procedure is:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{EHB algorithm} \citep{Engdahl1998}: an iterative relocation
        using the ak135 reference velocity model \citep{Kennett1995} with
        improved phase identification, particularly for $pP$ and $sP$ depth
        phases.  The EHB procedure reduced systematic depth biases in the
        pre-1990 bulletin by 10--30\,km for subduction-zone events
        \citep{Engdahl1998}.

  \item \textbf{ISC location algorithm} \citep{Bondár2011}: fixes the depth
        output from EHB and further reduces systematic hypocentre bias by
        modelling the spatial correlation of travel-time prediction errors
        using an empirical covariance function estimated from the bulletin's
        residual distribution.
\end{enumerate}

The ISC-authored solution is designated \emph{prime} when at least 6 defining
phases are available and the azimuthal gap is $< 180^\circ$.  Where these
criteria are not met, the highest-ranked contributing agency solution is used
as prime \citep{ISCGEM2018}.

Crucially, \emph{all} agency solutions are retained as secondary (non-prime)
hypocentres in the ISC bulletin.  This traceability model — derived from the
practice of the original ISS — ensures that all downstream applications can
reproduce the selection logic and compare solutions across agencies
\citep{ISCBulletinRebuild2020}.

\subsubsection{Magnitude assembly in ISC-GEM}

ISC-GEM reports a single $M_\mathrm{w}$ per event using the following protocol
\citep{ISCGEM2018}:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Direct $M_\mathrm{w}$} from global moment-tensor \catalogs:
        GCMT \citep{Dziewonski1981, Ekstrom2012} (preferred),
        USGS W-phase, EMSC MT, GeoNet CMT, ETH CMT.

  \item \textbf{$M_\mathrm{w}$ proxy from $M_\mathrm{s}$}: the ISC re-computes
        $M_\mathrm{s}$ using an \emph{alpha-trimmed median} (10\,\% trimming on
        each tail) of individual station amplitudes, suppressing outliers from
        poorly calibrated stations.  The resulting $M_\mathrm{s}$ is converted
        to $M_\mathrm{w}$ via an exponential (non-linear) regression.

  \item \textbf{$M_\mathrm{w}$ proxy from $m_\mathrm{b}$}: similarly trimmed
        and converted via a non-linear regression that accounts for
        $m_\mathrm{b}$ saturation above $\approx M_\mathrm{w}$\,6.2.
\end{enumerate}

The alpha-trimmed median is a robust location estimator in the statistical
sense: its breakdown point (the fraction of outliers that can corrupt the
estimate) is 10\,\%, compared with 0\,\% for the simple mean
\citep{Huber1981}.  For the station-level amplitude data used to compute
$M_\mathrm{s}$, where a single poorly calibrated station can deviate by
1--2 magnitude units, this robustness is practically important.

\subsection{Magnitude Homogenisation: Theory and Practice}
\label{sec:maghomog}

\subsubsection{The physical basis for moment magnitude}

Moment magnitude $M_\mathrm{w}$ was introduced by \citet{HanksKanamori1979} as
a logarithmic measure of the seismic moment $M_0$ (measured in dyne-cm):

\begin{equation}
  M_\mathrm{w} = \tfrac{2}{3}(\log_{10} M_0 - 16.1).
  \label{eq:mwdef}
\end{equation}

The seismic moment $M_0 = \mu A \bar{D}$, where $\mu$ is the shear modulus,
$A$ is the fault rupture area, and $\bar{D}$ is the mean slip, is a
physically meaningful measure of earthquake size that does not saturate at
large magnitudes \citep{Kanamori1977}.  In contrast, $m_\mathrm{b}$ saturates
above $M_\mathrm{w} \approx 6.0$--6.2 (because short-period body waves become
insensitive to the growing fault area), and $M_\mathrm{s}$ saturates above
$M_\mathrm{w} \approx 8.0$--8.2 \citep{Bormann2012}.

The saturation problem directly affects merging: a network reporting
$m_\mathrm{b} = 6.5$ for an $M_\mathrm{w}$\,7.5 event is not recording a
different earthquake; it is simply using a saturated scale.  Magnitude-based
filtering or association thresholds that do not account for scale-dependent
saturation will therefore misclassify or incorrectly reject legitimate matches
\citep{Bormann2012, ISCGEM2018}.

\subsubsection{Regression method: orthogonal vs.\ ordinary least squares}
\label{sec:regression}

The fundamental flaw in using Ordinary Least Squares (OLS) for magnitude
conversion is that OLS minimises the sum of squared vertical residuals,
implicitly assuming the predictor variable is measured without error.  When
both the predictor $M_x$ and the response $M_\mathrm{w}$ carry measurement
error of comparable magnitude, OLS produces a biased slope estimator
\citep{Fuller1987}:

\begin{equation}
  \hat{\beta}_{\mathrm{OLS}} = \beta \cdot \frac{\sigma_{M_x}^2}{\sigma_{M_x}^2 + \sigma_{\varepsilon}^2},
  \label{eq:olsbias}
\end{equation}

where $\beta$ is the true slope, $\sigma_{M_x}^2$ is the variance of the
true predictor, and $\sigma_{\varepsilon}^2$ is the measurement error variance.
The ratio is always $\leq 1$, so OLS systematically underestimates the true
conversion slope — an \emph{attenuation bias} that compresses the converted
magnitude range and biases $b$-value estimates from the homogenised \catalog\
\citep{Grunthal2016, Carroll2019}.

\paragraph{Generalised Orthogonal Regression.}
The solution is \textbf{Generalised Orthogonal Regression} (GOR), which
minimises the sum of squared perpendicular distances weighted by the ratio
$\eta = \sigma_{M_\mathrm{w}}^2 / \sigma_{M_x}^2$
\citep{Grunthal2016, Akkar2014, Cotton2013}.  The GOR slope is:

\begin{equation}
  \hat{\beta}_{\mathrm{GOR}} = \frac{
    (\mathit{SYY} - \eta\,\mathit{SXX}) + \sqrt{(\mathit{SYY} - \eta\,\mathit{SXX})^2 + 4\eta\,\mathit{SXY}^2}
  }{2\,\mathit{SXY}},
  \label{eq:gorslope}
\end{equation}

where $\mathit{SXX}$, $\mathit{SYY}$, $\mathit{SXY}$ are the corrected sums of
squares and cross-products.  When $\eta = 1$ (equal uncertainties), GOR reduces
to the standard geometric mean (GM) regression; when $\eta \to 0$
(predictor error-free), GOR approaches OLS; and when $\eta \to \infty$
(response error-free), GOR approaches the reverse OLS regression of $M_x$ on
$M_\mathrm{w}$ \citep{Fuller1987}.

The SHEEC (SHARE European Earthquake Catalogue) project \citep{Grunthal2016}
provides published GOR coefficients for $M_\mathrm{L}$, $M_\mathrm{s}$, and
$m_\mathrm{b}$ to $M_\mathrm{w}$ conversion for European seismicity, estimated
from the overlap period of the EMS98 national \catalogs\ and the ISC-GEM
dataset.  These coefficients are recommended as defaults for European and
Mediterranean merging applications \citep{Grunthal2016}.

\paragraph{Non-linear functional forms.}
For $m_\mathrm{b}$ and $M_\mathrm{s}$, the ISC-GEM pipeline
\citep{ISCGEM2018} chose exponential (non-linear) conversion models rather than
linear GOR, on the basis that the physical saturation of both scales introduces
a non-linear relationship at the extremes that linear regression cannot capture.
Piecewise-linear models with optimised crossover points — as implemented in
the CPTI15 Italian \catalog\ pipeline using the \citet{Gasperini2012} framework
— offer a practical middle ground that approximates non-linearity without the
extrapolation risks of exponential models.

\paragraph{The \citet{Scordilis2006} coefficients.}
The widely used Scordilis (\citeyear{Scordilis2006}) linear relationships
(e.g.\ $M_\mathrm{w} = 0.67\,M_\mathrm{L} + 1.17$,
$M_\mathrm{w} = 0.85\,m_\mathrm{b} + 1.03$,
$M_\mathrm{w} = 0.67\,M_\mathrm{s} + 2.07$ for $M_\mathrm{s} < 6.2$)
are OLS regressions on a global dataset.  As such they carry the attenuation
bias in \eqref{eq:olsbias} and are known to systematically underestimate
$M_\mathrm{w}$ for large events near each scale's saturation boundary
\citep{Grunthal2016}.  They are appropriate as a first approximation and for
applications where the bias is small compared to other uncertainties, but should
be replaced by GOR-derived relationships for research-grade \catalogs.

\subsubsection{Magnitude type priority hierarchy}
\label{sec:maghierarchy}

The ISC-GEM priority order \citep{ISCGEM2018}, consistent with
\citet{Grunthal2016} and the ISC bulletin operational practice, is:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item $M_\mathrm{w}$ from GCMT waveform inversion \citep{Dziewonski1981,
        Ekstrom2012} — gold standard; does not require conversion.
  \item $M_\mathrm{w}$ from USGS/NEIC W-phase or broadband CMT solution.
  \item $M_\mathrm{w}$ from regional CMT (GeoNet \citep{GeoNetEqCatalogue},
        INGV, GEOFON/GFZ, ETH).
  \item $M_\mathrm{w}$ proxy from $M_\mathrm{s}$ (preferred over $m_\mathrm{b}$
        because $M_\mathrm{s}$ saturates at higher magnitude and the conversion
        scatter is smaller \citep{Bormann2012}).
  \item $M_\mathrm{w}$ proxy from $m_\mathrm{b}$.
  \item $M_\mathrm{w}$ proxy from $M_\mathrm{L}$ (most region-dependent;
        highly variable station corrections).
  \item $M_\mathrm{w}$ proxy from $M_\mathrm{d}$ or $M_\mathrm{c}$ (duration /
        coda magnitude; least reliable, used only when nothing else is
        available).
\end{enumerate}

Within each priority tier, the estimate with lower reported uncertainty is
preferred \citep{ISCGEM2018}.  Where no uncertainty is reported, network
authority from \cref{tab:hierarchy} acts as a tie-breaker.

\subsubsection{Bayesian magnitude merging across networks}

Simple priority selection discards valid information when multiple authoritative
$M_\mathrm{w}$ estimates exist for the same event.
\citet{Schorlemmer2024} proposed a Bayesian framework in which each network $i$'s
observed magnitude is modelled as a linear function of the unknown true magnitude
$M_\mathrm{true}$:

\begin{equation}
  M_{\mathrm{obs},i} = a_i + b_i\,M_{\mathrm{true}} + \varepsilon_i,
  \qquad \varepsilon_i \sim \mathcal{N}(0,\,\sigma_i^2),
  \label{eq:bayesmag}
\end{equation}

where $a_i$ and $b_i$ are network-specific intercept and slope (estimated from
the overlap period), and $\sigma_i^2$ is the within-network reporting variance.
A Gutenberg-Richter prior $p(M_\mathrm{true}) \propto 10^{-b M_\mathrm{true}}$
is placed on the true magnitude.  The posterior mean is:

\begin{equation}
  \hat{M}_\mathrm{true} = \frac{
    \sum_i b_i (M_{\mathrm{obs},i} - a_i)/\sigma_i^2
    - b \ln(10) \cdot \sigma_\mathrm{post}^2
  }{
    \sum_i b_i^2/\sigma_i^2
  },
  \label{eq:bayesposterior}
\end{equation}

where $\sigma_\mathrm{post}^2 = (\sum_i b_i^2/\sigma_i^2)^{-1}$ is the
posterior variance.  When two authoritative solutions agree within
$0.1\,M_\mathrm{w}$ units, the posterior is insensitive to the choice of $b$;
when they disagree by $> 0.3\,M_\mathrm{w}$ units, a data quality flag should
be raised regardless of which method is used \citep{Schorlemmer2024}.

\paragraph{Uncertainty propagation for converted magnitudes.}
When $M_\mathrm{w}$ is estimated via conversion from $M_x$, the total
uncertainty is \citep{DeadSea2022, Grunthal2016}:

\begin{equation}
  \sigma_{M_\mathrm{w}}^2 = \sigma_{\hat{\alpha}}^2
    + \left(\frac{\partial f}{\partial M_x}\right)^2 \sigma_{M_x}^2
    + \sigma_{\mathrm{model}}^2,
  \label{eq:uncert}
\end{equation}

where $\hat{\alpha}$ is the conversion-equation intercept (with uncertainty
$\sigma_{\hat{\alpha}}^2$ from the GOR fit), $f(M_x)$ is the conversion
function, $\sigma_{M_x}^2$ is the individual measurement uncertainty of the
predictor magnitude, and $\sigma_{\mathrm{model}}^2$ is the residual scatter
of the regression (model uncertainty).  For the Scordilis
$m_\mathrm{b} \to M_\mathrm{w}$ conversion with slope $\approx 0.85$, the
conversion amplifies the input uncertainty by a factor $0.85^2 \approx 0.72$
(a reduction), but the model residual ($\sigma_{\mathrm{model}} \approx 0.3$)
typically dominates \citep{Scordilis2006, Grunthal2016}.

\subsubsection{Combining macroseismic and instrumental estimates}

Where both macroseismic ($M_{\mathrm{w,macro}}$, derived from intensity data
via \citealt{Grunthal1998}) and instrumental ($M_{\mathrm{w,inst}}$) estimates
exist—a common situation for the historical period 1900--1960—the CPTI15
Italian pipeline \citep{CPTI15} uses inverse-variance weighting:

\begin{equation}
  M_{\mathrm{w,comb}} = \frac{
    M_{\mathrm{w,macro}} / \sigma_{\mathrm{macro}}^2
    + M_{\mathrm{w,inst}} / \sigma_{\mathrm{inst}}^2
  }{
    1/\sigma_{\mathrm{macro}}^2 + 1/\sigma_{\mathrm{inst}}^2
  }.
  \label{eq:ivw}
\end{equation}

This is the minimum-variance unbiased estimator under the assumption of
independent Gaussian errors \citep{Kay1993}.  For the Italian \catalog,
$\sigma_{\mathrm{macro}} \approx 0.3$--0.5 and
$\sigma_{\mathrm{inst}} \approx 0.1$--0.2 \citep{CPTI15}, so the combined
estimate is dominated by the instrumental solution when both are available but
gracefully falls back to the macroseismic estimate for events with no
instrumental record.

\subsection{Network Authority Hierarchies and Regional Overrides}
\label{sec:authority}

\subsubsection{The philosophical distinction: selection vs.\ re-analysis}

Two fundamentally different philosophies underlie network authority models:

\begin{description}
  \item[Selection model (ANSS ComCat).] Each contributing network submits its
        preferred solution.  A weight is assigned to each network per product
        type; the highest-weight solution is the ``preferred'' product
        \citep{usgs_comcat}.  Where weights are equal, the most recently updated
        solution takes precedence.  No re-analysis is performed by the
        aggregator.

  \item[Re-analysis model (ISC).] The aggregator receives all raw phase data
        and performs its own relocation and magnitude computation using all
        contributed data simultaneously.  The aggregator's solution is prime;
        contributing solutions are retained as secondary
        \citep{ISCBulletinRebuild2020}.
\end{description}

For research-grade applications, the re-analysis model is strongly preferred
because it provides a single uniform solution computed with a common algorithm
and velocity model, eliminating inter-agency biases \citep{Storchak2013}.  For
rapid operational applications where relocation latency is unacceptable, the
selection model is appropriate.

\subsubsection{Regional authority and the ``authoritative network'' principle}

The ANSS ComCat implements the \emph{authoritative network} principle: for
earthquakes occurring within a defined geographic footprint, the regional
specialist network is given the highest preferred weight, overriding the NEIC
global solution \citep{usgs_comcat}.  Examples include the Caltech/USGS Southern
California Seismic Network (SCSN, code \texttt{ci}) for Southern California,
the University of Utah Seismograph Stations (\texttt{uu}) for the Intermountain
West, and GeoNet (\texttt{nz}) for Aotearoa New Zealand \citep{GeoNetEqCatalogue}.

The justification is that regional networks operate denser station arrays with
local velocity models specifically calibrated to regional structure, producing
lower-uncertainty locations and completeness to smaller magnitudes than any
global agency \citep{Woessner2005, Hutton2010}.  For the New Zealand region,
\citet{GeoNetEqCatalogue} document that GeoNet solutions achieve completeness to
approximately $M_\mathrm{L}$\,1.5 in the North Island, compared to the ISC's
practical completeness limit of $\approx M$\,4.5 for the same region.

\subsubsection{Temporal variation in network authority}

Network capabilities change over time: networks expand or contract, recording
equipment is upgraded, velocity models are revised.  The authority hierarchy
should therefore be applied with awareness of the recording era.  For example,
the GCMT \catalog\ begins in 1976 \citep{Dziewonski1981}; for events before
that date, the tier-1 authority falls to ISC or NEIC.  GeoNet's digital
broadband network was not fully operational until the early 2000s; for
pre-2000 New Zealand events, ISC or the GNS analogue bulletin should be used
as the primary source \citep{GeoNetEqCatalogue}.

\subsection{Regional Catalogue Merging: Case Studies}
\label{sec:casestudies}

\subsubsection{Dead Sea Transform region}

\citet{DeadSea2022} described an automated pipeline for the Dead Sea Transform
region (DST-CAT), covering 1900--2017 and $M_\mathrm{w} \geq 3.0$, assembled
from ISC, the Geophysical Institute of Israel (GII), and several regional
networks.  Key methodological choices were:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Association}: 60\,s / 100\,km window with manual review of
        borderline cases.
  \item \textbf{Preferred solution}: specialist regional agency (GII) for
        events within the DST zone; ISC for the broader region; GCMT where
        available.
  \item \textbf{Magnitude conversion}: GOR-based $M_\mathrm{L}$, $M_\mathrm{s}$,
        and $m_\mathrm{b}$ to $M_\mathrm{w}$ relationships derived from the
        1976--2017 overlap period, with uncertainties propagated via
        \eqref{eq:uncert}.
  \item \textbf{Era-dependent windows}: wider windows (90\,s / 150\,km) for
        the pre-1960 period to account for analogue timing errors.
\end{itemize}

The study explicitly noted the failure of fixed-window methods during the 1995
$M_\mathrm{w}$\,7.2 Aqaba mainshock sequence, where aftershock rates exceeded
10 events per hour for two days, and adopted a hybrid approach with manual
review for the highest-rate periods.

\subsubsection{Mexico unified catalogue}

\citet{Zuniga2019} assembled a unified Mexican seismicity \catalog\
(1750--2018, $M \geq 3$) from seven source \catalogs\ including the SSN
(Servicio Sismológico Nacional), NEIC PDE, ISC, and the Reyes-Dávila regional
bulletins.  The deduplication procedure used a 60\,s / $1^\circ$ ($\approx$
111\,km) window and a source priority list with the SSN as authoritative for
events within Mexico.  The study documents a magnitude change point in 1963
(transition from analogue to digital recording) requiring separate
completeness analyses for the two periods.

\subsubsection{Turkey homogeneous catalogue}

\citet{Tan2021} constructed a homogeneous $M_\mathrm{w}$ \catalog\ for Turkey
(1900--2018) from TURKNET, AFAD, ISC, GCMT, and several historical sources.
The study used a 60\,s / 100\,km proximity window and applied GOR conversions
with era-specific coefficients.  A key finding was that the Scordilis OLS
coefficients produced a systematic overestimate of $M_\mathrm{w}$ for Turkish
$M_\mathrm{L}$ values below 3.5, by approximately 0.2\,units — confirming the
OLS attenuation bias described in \cref{sec:regression}.  The homogenised
\catalog\ was then declustered using the \citet{Reasenberg1985} algorithm
before use in seismic hazard calculations.

\subsubsection{Italy: CPTI15}

The Parametric Catalogue of Italian Earthquakes (CPTI15, \citealt{CPTI15})
is the most comprehensive regional merging effort for a seismically active
European country, covering 1000--2014 with $M_\mathrm{w} \geq 4.0$ for the
instrumental period and extending to smaller magnitudes and earlier periods
for historical events.  It merges multiple national bulletins
(INGV, GNDT, ING), the ISC bulletin, GCMT solutions, and a rich archive of
macroseismic intensity data \citep{DBMI15}.

CPTI15 uses a piecewise-linear GOR framework \citep{Gasperini2012} for
magnitude conversion and the inverse-variance-weighted combination of
\eqref{eq:ivw} for events with both instrumental and macroseismic estimates.
The catalogue is tied to the INGV ShakeMap intensity model to ensure
consistency with ground-motion prediction equations used in Italian national
hazard maps \citep{Stucchi2011}.

\subsubsection{Aotearoa New Zealand}

The GeoNet \catalog\ \citep{GeoNetEqCatalogue} is the authoritative source for
New Zealand seismicity, providing real-time and reviewed solutions from the
national seismograph network.  \citet{Illsley2025} recently produced a new
high-precision \catalog\ for the Taupō Volcanic Zone (TVZ) by re-processing
GeoNet waveforms with a template-matching algorithm \citep{Chamberlain2018},
demonstrating that merged \catalogs\ for New Zealand should treat the TVZ and
broader crustal seismicity as distinct populations with different completeness
thresholds.

The National Seismic Hazard Model \citep{NSHM2022BSSA} integrates the GeoNet
\catalog\ with paleo-seismic and geological data, requiring careful
synchronisation of magnitude scales: GeoNet reports primarily $M_\mathrm{L}$
for small events and $M_\mathrm{w}$ for larger events, but the transition
between scales is not uniformly applied across the \catalog's recording history
\citep{GeoNetLocationInfo}.  For input to ground-motion models, all magnitudes
must be expressed in $M_\mathrm{w}$, necessitating the conversion procedures
discussed in \cref{sec:maghomog}.

\subsection{Parameter Selection Within a Merged Group}
\label{sec:selection}

Once event groups have been identified by one of the methods in
\cref{sec:matching}, parameter selection determines the single origin time,
hypocentre, and magnitude that will represent the physical earthquake in the
merged \catalog.

\subsubsection{Hypocenter and origin time}

The preferred hypocentre is selected from the contributing solutions according
to the priority order in \cref{tab:hierarchy}, subject to quality thresholds.
The ISC-GEM criteria for accepting a prime hypocentre
\citep{ISCGEM2018, Bondár2011} are:

\begin{itemize}
  \item Azimuthal gap $< 180^\circ$,
  \item At least 6 defining phases,
  \item Free depth (not fixed at a default value),
  \item RMS travel-time residual $< 2.0$\,s.
\end{itemize}

The origin time is taken from the selected hypocentre \emph{without averaging}.
Averaging origin times across agencies is incorrect because agencies use
different phase sets and velocity models; their origin times are not independent
estimates of a common truth but are each internally consistent with their
respective location procedures \citep{Storchak2013}.

Where no single agency meets all criteria, the highest-priority solution is
used with a downgraded quality flag, and the criteria violation is recorded as
event metadata.

\subsubsection{Depth selection}

Among qualifying hypocentres, the solution with the smallest formal depth
uncertainty is preferred.  Where formal uncertainty is unavailable, the
solution with the largest number of depth-sensitive phases ($pP$, $sP$,
$pwP$, depth phases) is preferred.  Depth solutions fixed at standard values
(10, 33, 100\,km) are given lowest priority and flagged with
\texttt{depth\_type = fixed} \citep{Engdahl2020}.

For events with no free-depth solution from any contributing agency, the
depth should be reported as the fixed value of the best-available agency,
with explicit documentation of the fixing convention used.

\subsubsection{Magnitude selection and conversion}

Apply the priority hierarchy of \cref{sec:maghierarchy}.  When multiple
estimates exist at the same priority tier and formal uncertainties are
available, the Bayesian posterior mean \eqref{eq:bayesposterior} is the
optimal estimator \citep{Schorlemmer2024}.  The alpha-trimmed median
\citep{ISCGEM2018} is an acceptable substitute when the Bayesian framework
cannot be calibrated (e.g.\ for sparse regional \catalogs\ without sufficient
overlap for network intercept estimation).  When neither is applicable,
select the estimate from the highest-priority agency and document the choice.

\subsubsection{Focal mechanism}

For events where focal mechanisms are available from multiple sources, the
following priority order is recommended based on the ISC-GEM protocol and
established reliability assessments \citep{Dziewonski1981, Ekstrom2012}:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item GCMT (waveform centroid moment tensor);
  \item USGS/NEIC broadband CMT or W-phase solution;
  \item GEOFON/GFZ regional moment tensor;
  \item GeoNet CMT (for New Zealand events, \citealt{GeoNetEqCatalogue});
  \item INGV CMT (for Italian events);
  \item First-motion polarity solution with $\geq 20$ station polarities;
  \item Automated first-motion solution.
\end{enumerate}

Within each tier, rank by: (i) variance reduction (higher is better),
(ii) station polarity count (more is better), (iii) misfit (lower is better).
All available mechanisms should be retained as supplementary products to
enable downstream comparison studies \citep{ISCBulletinRebuild2020}.

\subsection{Declustering and Its Relationship to Merging}
\label{sec:declustering}

Declustering — the removal of aftershocks and foreshocks to obtain a Poissonian
background catalogue — is a separate post-processing step from merging but is
closely related in practice.  Two points of interaction are important.

\paragraph{Merging before declustering.}
Declustering algorithms (e.g.\ \citealt{GardnerKnopoff1974, Reasenberg1985,
Zaliapin2013}) require an internally consistent, homogeneous magnitude scale and
a complete event listing to correctly identify cluster members.  If two \catalogs\
are merged \emph{after} independent declustering, cluster members that appear
in only one source \catalog\ may be lost, biasing the background rate estimate
\citep{Hainzl2016}.  The correct order is: merge first (retaining all events
from all sources), then decluster.

\paragraph{Duplicate detection vs.\ foreshock-aftershock discrimination.}
The nearest-neighbour proximity function (\cref{sec:nn}) was introduced
specifically to address the case where duplicate detection and aftershock
identification are conflated \citep{Tanaka2022}.  This is a real operational
hazard: aftershocks occurring within seconds of the mainshock are easily
misidentified as duplicates by fixed-window methods during the most intense
phase of a sequence.  Systems that merge and decluster simultaneously (e.g.\
using a single proximity window) can lose genuine aftershocks and overcount
the mainshock, distorting both the $b$-value and the Omori-law parameters of
the sequence \citep{Hainzl2016, Reasenberg1985}.

\subsection{Data Exchange Standards and Provenance}
\label{sec:standards}

\subsubsection{QuakeML and FDSN}

The QuakeML 1.2 schema \citep{QuakeML} is the de facto standard for representing
earthquake event metadata in XML.  It natively supports the representation of
multiple competing origin solutions, multiple magnitude estimates per origin,
focal mechanism solutions with full moment tensors, and phase arrival data.
The \texttt{preferredOriginID} and \texttt{preferredMagnitudeID} attributes
are the schema's mechanism for expressing a selection decision, while retaining
all contributing solutions as first-class objects.

FDSN event web services \citep{USGSFDSN} provide HTTP access to QuakeML records
from participating agencies, enabling automated ingestion of source \catalogs\
in a standard format.  For merging systems that ingest from multiple FDSN nodes
simultaneously, the \texttt{contributor} and \texttt{author} fields within each
QuakeML origin provide the information needed to implement the same-agency rule
(\cref{sec:sameagency}) \citep{QuakeML, USGSFDSN}.

\subsubsection{Provenance tracking}

Any merged \catalog\ intended for research use should preserve the full
derivation chain of each event record.  The W3C PROV data model
\citep{PROVO} provides a formal language for expressing provenance graphs.
For earthquake \catalogs, provenance records should document:

\begin{itemize}
  \item The source \catalog\ identifier and version for each contributing record;
  \item The association algorithm and threshold values used;
  \item The selection rule applied for each parameter type (origin, magnitude,
        focal mechanism);
  \item The conversion equation and coefficients applied to any magnitude
        requiring homogenisation, with uncertainty estimates.
\end{itemize}

The FAIR data principles \citep{FAIRPrinciples2016} require that merged
\catalogs\ be assigned persistent identifiers (DOIs) with version tracking
\citep{DataCiteVersioning}.  Where the input \catalogs\ have their own DOIs,
the provenance record should cite them explicitly to enable users to trace
any merged event back to its source records \citep{GOFAIR}.

\subsubsection{The same-agency rule}
\label{sec:sameagency}

If the same network contributes two records to a single merged group, the group
almost certainly spans two distinct physical earthquakes.  This is because any
competent network applies intra-network deduplication upstream of its data
submission; consequently, two records from the same network in the same time-
distance window represent genuinely distinct detected events, not two reports
of one event \citep{Benz2019}.  The group should be dissolved and a secondary
pairwise pass performed on its members, constrained by the same-agency rule as
in the ILP constraint \eqref{eq:ilp}.

\subsubsection{Failed-group recovery}

When a group fails validation (e.g.\ because of a same-agency clash or
anomalous magnitude range), the correct response is not to emit all members as
singletons, but to attempt a constrained re-grouping.  A one-level secondary
greedy pass within the failed group — where the same-agency rule is enforced as
a hard constraint — can recover valid sub-groups \citep{Benz2019, Tanaka2022}.
Sub-groups that still fail validation after the secondary pass are emitted as
singletons with a QC flag recording the failed-group origin.

\subsubsection{Magnitude completeness audit}
\label{sec:mc}

The magnitude of completeness $M_c$ should be estimated for the merged \catalog\
in rolling temporal bins (5-year windows are recommended by \citealt{Woessner2005})
using either the Maximum Curvature (MAXC) or Goodness-of-Fit (GFT) method
\citep{Wiemer2000}.  Events below $M_c$ must be flagged as potentially incomplete
in any bin where $M_c$ is elevated and excluded from $b$-value or seismicity rate
calculations that assume completeness \citep{Aki1965}.  Sudden upward steps in
$M_c$ correspond to network deterioration or data gaps and should be documented
alongside the network history that explains them \citep{Woessner2005}.

The single highest-priority gap is the regression methodology.  Replacing
linear OLS \citet{Scordilis2006} coefficients with GOR-derived relationships
\citep{Grunthal2016} would reduce systematic bias in the converted
frequency-magnitude distribution without requiring architectural changes.  For
applications in the New Zealand region specifically, region-calibrated
$M_\mathrm{L} \to M_\mathrm{w}$ GOR coefficients estimated from the GeoNet
and ISC-GEM overlap period (1976--present) should be derived and used in
preference to global coefficients \citep{GeoNetLocationInfo, NSHM2022BSSA}.

The second highest-priority gap is the adoption of a Union-Find graph
association step (\cref{sec:graph}).  The current greedy forward-sweep misses
transitive associations that arise when an intermediate-time event B links two
events A and C that would not independently satisfy the proximity criterion.
A Union-Find graph implementation resolves this in $O(n\,\alpha(n))$ time with
no change to the downstream parameter-selection logic.

\begin{abstract}
We present the \emph{Catalogue of Catalogues} (CofC), an open-source web
platform for ingesting, quality-scoring, merging, and statistically
analysing earthquake catalogues from multiple agencies and formats, following
the FAIR data principles (Findable, Accessible, Interoperable, Reusable).  CofC accepts data as CSV/TXT, GeoJSON,
QuakeML~1.2 Basic Event Description (BED), and the FDSN Event Web Service.
A six-strategy merging engine associates duplicate reports one to one (each
report with its closest counterpart in every other catalogue) within configurable,
adaptive time and distance windows (widened for larger and deeper events), with
magnitude and depth consistency enforced as separate per-group gates, and
resolves conflicts by solution quality, source priority, most recent solution,
record completeness, or an averaged or median epicentre, with optional per-field
rules for depth, magnitude and focal mechanism and the option to hold flagged
groups for review, keeping every contributing report as per-event lineage.  A transparent quality-scoring system (a 0--100 index with
letter grades) evaluates each event across five dimensions: location precision,
network geometry, solution fit, magnitude quality, and analyst-evaluation status.
Standard seismological analyses run client-side in the browser:
Gutenberg-Richter $b$-value estimation by maximum likelihood, magnitude of
completeness ($M_c$) by $b$-value stability (the default), a goodness-of-fit test
or Maximum Curvature (MAXC),
Gardner-Knopoff declustering, and cumulative seismic moment and energy.  A fully
synthetic two-catalogue New Zealand example (no real event data), processed end to
end by the platform's own engine, illustrates the workflow and quantifies the
biases an analyst must control: the matcher pairs 93\% of the earthquakes both
agencies report, with 98\% of its pairs correct; a quality threshold removes 95\%
of the events with azimuthal gap above $180^{\circ}$ but also favours larger
events, lowering $b$ by 0.06; the MAXC and goodness-of-fit estimates of $M_c$
fall well below the completeness of the merged catalogue, which the more
conservative $b$-value-stability estimate reaches; and window declustering lowers
$b$ by a further 0.12.
Above a stable cut-off, the estimator recovers the planted $b = 1.00$ of the
independent events ($1.01 \pm 0.01$).  These values characterise the controlled
synthetic inputs, not real New Zealand seismicity.
We identify six categories of statistical bias that affect merged-catalogue
analysis and give practical mitigation guidance.  Source code and
documentation are freely available under the MIT licence.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

\subsection{The role of earthquake catalogues in seismology}

Earthquake catalogues are the observational basis of quantitative
seismology.  Methods from probabilistic seismic hazard analysis (PSHA;
\citealt{cornell1968}; e.g.\ the 2022 Aotearoa New Zealand NSHM,
\citealt{gerstenberger2024}) through operational earthquake forecasting
\citep{schorlemmer2007} to tectonic geodesy all rest on the assumption that the
catalogue faithfully represents the space-time-magnitude distribution of
seismicity.  The
Gutenberg-Richter (GR) frequency-magnitude relation \citep{gutenberg1944}
and its $b$-value, which encodes the relative proportion of small to large
earthquakes \citep{kagan1999,schorlemmer2005}, are estimated directly from
catalogue data; any systematic error in the catalogue propagates directly
into hazard and risk assessments.  Similarly, the Epidemic-Type Aftershock
Sequence (ETAS) model \citep{ogata1988}, the most widely used stochastic
framework for short-term earthquake forecasting, requires a complete,
Poissonian background catalogue that is free from both incompleteness and
aftershock contamination.

The same region is often covered by several catalogues from different
providers, each built with different station networks, velocity models,
magnitude scales, analysis procedures, and update frequencies.  Examples include
GeoNet (New Zealand; \citealt{petersen2011}), the Northern California Earthquake
Data Center \citep{ncedc2014}, the International Seismological Centre (ISC;
\citealt{storchak2013}), and the Engdahl-van der Hilst-Buland relocated global
catalogue \citep{engdahl1998}.  Researchers routinely need to
\emph{merge} or \emph{compare} these sources: to extend a short modern
catalogue with historical data, to supplement a global catalogue with a
higher-resolution regional one, or to construct a unified input for PSHA
over a region monitored by multiple agencies.  This multi-source workflow
introduces three classes of problem that, if ignored, produce statistically
invalid results.

\subsection{Problem 1: Format heterogeneity}

No universally adopted exchange format exists for seismological data.  The
QuakeML~1.2 Basic Event Description (BED) schema \citep{schorlemmer2011}
provides an XML vocabulary covering origins, magnitudes, focal
mechanisms, picks, arrivals, and amplitude measurements, and is the standard
output of the FDSN Event Web Service \citep{fdsn2013}.  However, many agency
archives remain in agency-specific CSV layouts, plain text bulletins, or
legacy binary formats.  The GeoJSON format \citep{geojson2016} has gained
traction for web-facing services but provides no controlled vocabulary for
seismological fields; column and property names vary freely between providers.
When a researcher assembles a multi-source dataset, the practical first step
is writing bespoke format-conversion code, a task that is repetitive,
error-prone, and rarely shared or version-controlled.

\subsection{Problem 2: Duplicate events and location scatter}

When two catalogues cover the same region, the same earthquake typically
appears in both, reported with slightly different origin times, hypocentres,
and magnitudes.  These differences arise from physical causes:
different station sets give different travel-time residuals; regional
versus global velocity models bias depth systematically; and automatic picks
differ from manually reviewed ones.  The global relocation literature
documents that, even for well-recorded teleseismic events, agency-to-agency
hypocentre differences commonly reach 10--20~km in epicentre and tens of
kilometres in depth, which is far less well constrained \citep{engdahl1998}.
Na{\"i}vely concatenating catalogues without duplicate removal counts every
earthquake that more than one agency reports once per report: it inflates $N(M)$
wherever the catalogues overlap, raises the apparent $a$-value, and distorts rate
estimates.  \citet{storchak2013} describe the
duplicate-association procedure developed for the ISC-GEM global
catalogue, which required multi-year expert review.  Automated, configurable
duplicate association is available in catalogue-building libraries
\citep{oqmbtk} and operational monitoring systems \citep{seiscomp2008}, but not
in a browser-based interface that a regional researcher can apply directly to
an arbitrary pair of uploaded catalogues without scripting or an operational
deployment.

\subsection{Problem 3: Statistical biases in merged catalogues}
\label{sec:biasmech}

Even after format conversion and duplicate removal, several statistical biases
require active mitigation before standard analyses are valid.  We summarise the
mechanisms of the four most consequential biases here; the full set of six,
their practical mitigation steps, and how CofC supports each are collected in
Section~\ref{sec:biases}.

\textbf{Magnitude of completeness ($M_c$).}  A catalogue is complete above
$M_c$, the smallest magnitude at which essentially all events are
detected by the network.  Below $M_c$, the GR relation breaks down
\citep{rydelek1989}.  Fitting $b$ without first determining and enforcing
$M_c$ leads to systematic underestimation \citep{woessner2005}.  Several
methods for estimating $M_c$ exist \citep{wiemer2000,
caogao2002, woessner2005, mignan2012}, with different assumptions and biases.
When merging two catalogues with different $M_c$ values
(e.g., $M_c = 1.5$ for a dense regional network and $M_c = 3.0$ for a global
catalogue), the combined FMD will be incomplete between the two thresholds
unless the analyst enforces the higher cut-off.

\textbf{Magnitude scale heterogeneity.}  Different agencies routinely use
different magnitude scales: local magnitude ($M_L$; \citealt{richter1935}),
body-wave magnitude ($m_b$), surface-wave magnitude ($M_s$), and moment
magnitude ($M_w$; \citealt{hanks1979,kanamori1977}).  Each scale saturates at
a different value ($M_L$ above $\sim 6.5$, $m_b$ above $\sim 6.0$;
\citealt{bormann2009}), and inter-scale regressions carry scatter that depends
on the regression method \citep{das2011,castellaro2006}.
Mixing scales inflates the apparent variance of the
FMD, artificially shifts $M_c$, and can change the estimated $b$ by up to
$\pm 0.2$ \citep{marzocchi2003}.  \citet{tinti1987} showed that, even with a
single scale, binning width and sample size both affect the precision of
$\hat{b}$.

\textbf{Aftershock clustering.}  Aftershock sequences violate the Poissonian
independence assumption underpinning the GR relation and ETAS background
estimation.  Aftershocks are most numerous at the small magnitudes just above
$M_c$, where they inflate event counts; because clustered aftershock
populations are commonly observed to carry a higher $b$-value than the background, failing to
decluster tends to bias the apparent $\hat{b}$ of the raw catalogue \emph{upward}
relative to the declustered (Poissonian) catalogue, although the sign and size
of the shift depend on the relative size distributions of the clustered and
background populations \citep{marsan2008}.  Declustering itself is not neutral
either: window methods remove small independent events more readily than large
ones and so bias the declustered $\hat{b}$ low \citep{mizrahi2021}.  We
illustrate both shifts directly in Section~\ref{sec:example}.  Several declustering algorithms
exist, differing in their sensitivity to swarm-type seismicity and to the
aftershocks of aftershocks \citep{vanstiphout2012}: the window method of
\citet{gardner1974}, the aftershock-zone scaling of \citet{kagan2002}, the
link-based method of \citet{reasenberg1985}, the ETAS-based stochastic method of
\citet{zhuang2002}, and the nearest-neighbour method of \citet{zaliapin2013}.
\citet{helmstetter2005} demonstrated that ignoring small-magnitude
aftershocks, often the majority of any sequence, underestimates cascading
stress transfer and therefore the effective rate of background seismicity.
The choice of declustering method and parameters is a major source of
uncertainty that is rarely propagated through to final hazard estimates.

\textbf{Location quality filtering.}  Events recorded by sparse networks have
large location uncertainties and poorly constrained depths.  If such events
are systematically concentrated at one magnitude or depth range, as is
typical for offshore events recorded by onshore networks, they introduce
spatial bias into $b$-value and $M_c$ mapping, and can bias an aggregate
Gutenberg-Richter fit where the location deficiency correlates with magnitude
via detection; a filter on location quality then removes small events
preferentially (Section~\ref{sec:example}).
\citet{bondar2004} showed that network geometry, in particular the
\emph{secondary} azimuthal gap, together with the number of stations and the
nearest-station distance, controls horizontal location accuracy, with large
gaps typically associated with errors of tens of kilometres.

\subsection{Existing tools and their limitations}
\label{sec:existing}

The standard desktop tool for catalogue-based seismicity analysis is
ZMAP \citep{wiemer2001}, which implements GR analysis, spatial $b$-value
mapping, declustering, and Omori-law fitting in a MATLAB environment.
It requires MATLAB licensing and
familiarity with its GUI; it can compare two catalogues to flag identical
events but does not reconcile them into a merged catalogue, offers no
quality-scored event selection, and does not read the QuakeML exchange format.  ObsPy \citep{obspy2010} provides a Python
framework for reading and writing QuakeML and performing basic catalogue
operations, but all analysis requires custom scripting.  EQcorrscan
\citep{chamberlain2018} enables template-matching and repeating-earthquake
detection but produces catalogues that then require separate quality
assessment and merging tools.  OpenQuake \citep{pagani2014} consumes
pre-processed catalogues for PSHA but provides no tools for the
preprocessing itself.  Neither ZMAP, ObsPy, nor OpenQuake offers a guided
workflow for avoiding the biases described above, nor a persistent
web-accessible database for storing and sharing processed catalogues.
The Community Online Resource for Statistical Seismicity Analysis
\citep[CORSSA;][]{naylor2010,mignan2012,vanstiphout2012,woessner2010}
provides peer-reviewed tutorials on these methods but no software platform.

The need for such integration is intensifying.  Template-matching and
machine-learning phase-detection workflows now routinely yield catalogues with
up to an order of magnitude more events than traditional analyst-reviewed
bulletins \citep{chamberlain2018,mousavi2022,illsley2025,michailos2025,chamberlain2025},
while every regional network continues to publish its own authoritative product.  A single study area may
therefore be covered by several catalogues that differ in completeness,
magnitude scale, and location quality and must be reconciled before analysis.
This proliferation is spatially concentrated: a companion inventory of New
Zealand earthquake catalogues (Figure~\ref{fig:coverage}) shows that
intensively studied areas, such as the Taup\=o Volcanic Zone, the northern
Hikurangi margin, and the central Alpine Fault, are each covered by many
overlapping regional catalogues (panel~b), while large offshore tracts,
Fiordland, and the far south remain thinly catalogued (panel~a).  Reconciling
these overlapping products into a single, quality-aware view is the task CofC
addresses.

Format conversion, duplicate association, completeness estimation, and
declustering are individually well understood and, in isolation, available in
existing tools.  The GEM OpenQuake Model Building
Toolkit \citep{oqmbtk} compiles and homogenises multi-source catalogues with
configurable space--time duplicate windows, declustering, and completeness
estimation; SeisComP \citep{seiscomp2008} performs automated multi-agency
origin association and merging within operational monitoring pipelines.  The
construction of magnitude-homogeneous catalogues from heterogeneous databases
is itself an established methodology \citep{weatherill2016}, and the PyCSEP toolkit
\citep{savran2022} provides catalogue processing and statistical forecast
evaluation in Python.  What is missing is therefore not the algorithms but a single,
\emph{browser-native, no-install} platform that chains ingest, configurable
merging, transparent per-event quality scoring, and bias-aware analysis behind
one interface, preserves provenance at every step, and is usable by a regional
researcher on an arbitrary pair of uploaded catalogues without scripting or a
configured monitoring deployment.  Table~\ref{tab:comparison} contrasts the

At the institutional level, major providers each publish their own catalogues
through FDSN-compliant web services: GeoNet \citep{petersen2011}, the USGS
Comprehensive Catalogue \citep[ComCat;][]{comcat}, the Swiss Seismological
Service, the ISC \citep{storchak2013}, and the EMSC.  The EPOS Seismology
Thematic Core Service coordinates access across European networks.  ComCat additionally provides a web search
interface and the \texttt{libcomcat} client for programmatic multi-source
retrieval.  However, these services expose individual (or centrally
pre-merged) agency products rather than providing tools for users to merge,
quality-score, and analyse combinations of arbitrary uploaded catalogues from
multiple sources.  Automated multi-agency event association has been studied for
global catalogues \citep{yeck2019}, as has Bayesian magnitude
merging across networks \citep{Si2024}, but these methods are not
available as general-purpose tools for regional researchers.  In New
Zealand specifically, \citet{warrensmith2025} conducted the first
systematic quantitative assessment of GeoNet location quality using
azimuthal gap and uncertainty benchmarks; CofC's quality index
(Section~\ref{sec:quality}) scores the same kinds of quantities, and that
assessment is a natural benchmark against which to calibrate it
(Section~\ref{sec:discussion}).  A high-resolution research catalogue for the
Taup\=o Volcanic Zone \citep{illsley2025} is an example of a specialised
regional product that a researcher would want to ingest alongside the national
catalogue.

\subsection{FAIR principles and reproducibility}
\label{sec:fair}

A second motivation for CofC is the move towards FAIR scientific
data: Findable, Accessible, Interoperable, and Reusable
\citep{wilkinson2016}.  \citet{wilkinson2016} codified these principles
to address a crisis of reproducibility in data-intensive science, the same
crisis that afflicts earthquake catalogue analysis when preprocessing steps
are undocumented, format conversions are ad hoc, and processed datasets are
not versioned or persistently identified.  The FAIR principles have since been
adopted as the basis for data infrastructure by the European Plate Observing
System (EPOS), which coordinates seismological data services across 24
European nations.  In Aotearoa New Zealand specifically, FAIR is
applied alongside the CARE Principles for Indigenous Data Governance
(Collective benefit, Authority to control, Responsibility, and Ethics;
\citealt{carroll2020}), which recognise M\=aori data-sovereignty
interests in nationally significant datasets; the governance and
access-control implications are developed in the companion white paper
\citep{graham2026fair}.

CofC applies the FAIR principles to earthquake
catalogue data:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
  \item \textbf{Findable.}  Every catalogue stored in CofC receives a
    stable internal identifier and is indexed by searchable catalogue-level
    metadata (name, institution, temporal and geographic coverage, magnitude
    range, event count, licence).  Globally unique persistent identifiers are
    not minted by the platform itself; a released catalogue is instead designed
    to be deposited with an external agency (e.g.\ DataCite or Zenodo) to
    obtain a citable DOI (see Versioning, Section~\ref{sec:architecture}).
  \item \textbf{Accessible.}  A REST API provides machine-readable access
    to catalogue metadata and event data in multiple formats (CSV, JSON,
    GeoJSON, KML, QuakeML) under role-based access control: catalogue listings
    and metadata can be browsed without signing in, while event data and exports
    require an account with the Viewer, Editor, or Administrator role; the
    intended national access model is detailed in the companion white paper
    \citep{graham2026fair}.
  \item \textbf{Interoperable.}  Data are normalised to a canonical internal
    schema and re-exportable in standard formats.  Event types, evaluation modes
    and evaluation statuses are stored in the QuakeML~1.2 BED enumerations
    \citep{schorlemmer2011}; magnitude types, for which QuakeML defines no
    controlled vocabulary, are kept as reported, since their case is meaningful
    ($m_B$ is not $m_b$).
  \item \textbf{Reusable.}  Every merged event keeps its lineage (the source
    catalogues and every contributing report, the merge strategy and parameters,
    and the quality score of the published solution), and every export carries
    it together with the catalogue's version and a SHA-256 checksum of the
    exported rows, so that a study can state, and a reader can verify, the data
    state it used.
\end{itemize}

The design of CofC was shaped by two national community workshops in
Aotearoa New Zealand (GSNZ 2023 and GSNZ 2024; \citealt{gsnzworkshop2023,
gsnzworkshop2024}) that brought together researchers from GNS Science,
Victoria University of Wellington, University of Canterbury, and industry.
Workshop feedback informed the metadata schema, merge configuration options,
quality-grading approach, and access-control model.  A companion white paper \citep{graham2026fair} provides the full
FAIR framework specification for the Aotearoa New Zealand national earthquake
catalogue infrastructure, including governance, licensing, metadata standards,
and role-based access control; the present paper focuses on the software
implementation and statistical-analysis workflow.

\subsection{The Catalogue of Catalogues}

CofC addresses these three catalogue-analysis problems, together with the
cross-cutting need for event-level quality assessment, in a single,
browser-accessible platform.  It ingests heterogeneous formats without
custom scripting, detects and removes duplicate events using configurable
thresholds grounded in the association standards of \citet{storchak2013}
and \citet{engdahl1998}, scores every event on a transparent quality index,
and presents GR analysis and declustering in a guided workflow that surfaces
the statistical assumptions at each step.  Built on Next.js
\citep{nextjs2024} and MongoDB \citep{mongodb2024}, it requires no local
installation and is deployable to any cloud provider or on-premises server.
The remainder of this paper describes the platform architecture
(Section~\ref{sec:architecture}), the merging engine
(Section~\ref{sec:merge}), the built-in statistical analyses
(Section~\ref{sec:analysis}), a worked New Zealand example
(Section~\ref{sec:example}), and detailed guidance on avoiding the most
consequential statistical biases (Section~\ref{sec:biases}).

\subsection{Contributions}
\label{sec:contributions}

The individual algorithms CofC employs are deliberately standard: maximum-likelihood
$b$-value estimation \citep{aki1965,utsu1965}, completeness estimation by $b$-value
stability \citep{caogao2002,woessner2005}, goodness of fit or maximum curvature
\citep{wiemer2000}, and Gardner-Knopoff \citep{gardner1974} and Reasenberg
\citep{reasenberg1985} declustering.  The contribution of this work is therefore not
a new estimator but the \emph{integration} of the multi-catalogue
workflow into a single open, web-native, provenance-preserving platform,
together with a transparent event-level quality index.  Specifically, the
contributions are:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
  \item \textbf{An integrated, browser-based workflow} spanning
    heterogeneous-format ingest, configurable duplicate detection, quality
    scoring, conflict-resolution merging, and standard seismicity analysis,
    requiring no local installation or licensed software
    (Table~\ref{tab:comparison}).  Catalogue-building libraries such as the
    GEM toolkit \citep{oqmbtk} and operational systems such as SeisComP
    \citep{seiscomp2008} cover much of the analytical chain, but to our
    knowledge none delivers it through a browser-native, no-install interface
    with an integrated per-event quality index.
  \item \textbf{A transparent, configurable event-level quality index}
    (equation~\eqref{eq:quality}): a five-dimension 0--100 score with letter
    grades.  Its components (location precision, network geometry, solution
    fit, magnitude quality, and evaluation status) are the standard
    determinants of solution quality used in agency review and ground-truth
    location-quality classification \citep{bondar2004,woessner2005,warrensmith2025};
    the contribution is their combination into a single reproducible,
    user-configurable index that is computed on import and exposed throughout a
    public web platform, rather than a new quality criterion.
  \item \textbf{Configurable, automated duplicate detection and conflict
    resolution} for arbitrary catalogue pairs, with six interchangeable merge
    strategies and physical-validity gates, generalising the expert-driven
    association procedures developed for global catalogues
    \citep{storchak2013,engdahl1998,yeck2019} into a tool regional researchers
    can apply themselves.
  \item \textbf{FAIR-by-design provenance}: every merged catalogue records its
    full per-event lineage (source-catalogue IDs, every contributing report and
    the one whose solution was published, merge strategy and thresholds, quality
    score, review status), re-exported as columns or properties in the CSV, JSON, GeoJSON and
    KML outputs and as a per-event QuakeML \texttt{comment}
    \citep{schorlemmer2011}, with the catalogue version and a checksum of the
    exported rows recorded in every export, operationalising the FAIR principles
    \citep{wilkinson2016} for routine catalogue work.
  \item \textbf{A bias-aware, guided analysis path} that surfaces the
    underlying statistical assumptions (completeness, magnitude-scale
    homogeneity, declustering, and quality filtering) at the point of use,
    consolidating guidance previously dispersed across the CORSSA tutorials
    \citep{naylor2010,mignan2012,vanstiphout2012} into an executable workflow
    (Section~\ref{sec:biases}).
\end{enumerate}

\section{Platform Architecture}
\label{sec:architecture}

CofC presents the catalogue workflow as a single guided pipeline
(Figure~\ref{fig:workflow}): heterogeneous inputs are ingested and normalised,
every event is quality-scored on import, duplicate events are detected and
resolved during merging, standard seismicity analyses are run in the browser,
and processed catalogues are re-exported in standard formats, with all
intermediate state and provenance persisted in the database at every step.

\subsection{Technology stack}

CofC follows a three-tier architecture: a React~18 / Next.js~15
\citep{nextjs2024} frontend served via the App Router, a serverless backend
layer of Next.js API routes, and a MongoDB \citep{mongodb2024} persistence
tier (server $\geq$\,6; accessed through the v7 Node.js driver).  All tiers communicate through a versioned REST API; no separate
application server is required.

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
  \item \textbf{Frontend:} React~18, TypeScript, Tailwind CSS, and a
    component library of 40$+$ shadcn/ui primitives for forms, tables,
    dialogs, and data-display cards.  Apache ECharts \citep{echarts2024}
    provides interactive time-series panels; Leaflet \citep{leaflet2024}
    provides the map layer.
  \item \textbf{Backend:} Next.js API routes deployed as serverless
    functions, enabling horizontal scaling without managing long-running
    processes.  Events are stored with indexed coordinate fields supporting
    bounding-box queries, including boxes that cross the antimeridian.
  \item \textbf{Parsers:} Custom TypeScript parsers for CSV/TXT, GeoJSON
    \citep{geojson2016}, and QuakeML~1.2 BED \citep{schorlemmer2011}.
    FDSN web-service integration imports live GeoNet data (chunked REST
    retrieval with automatic time-window subdivision for large queries) into a
    new catalogue, or into an existing GeoNet-import catalogue, where events are
    matched by their GeoNet event ID and only those whose fields changed are
    updated.  Records the agency flags as duplicate, not existing, or not
    locatable are excluded and counted; the agency's own event type is kept
    alongside the QuakeML one.
\end{itemize}

The application is containerised with Docker (Compose recipes for development
and production) and is designed to deploy to any cloud provider or
on-premises server.

\subsection{Data model}

Each \emph{catalogue} is a named container with metadata (name, description,
source agency, status, and a server-managed version).  Each \emph{event} record
stores the mandatory seismological fields (origin time, latitude, longitude,
depth, magnitude) plus about fifty optional fields drawn from QuakeML:
uncertainty estimates (including the error ellipse and its confidence level),
azimuthal gap, station and phase counts, magnitude type, evaluation mode and
status, agency ID, focal mechanisms, phase picks, arrivals, and amplitude
measurements.  All QuakeML complex sub-objects are stored as JSON strings so
that no information is discarded on import.  Every stored event also carries its
quality score and grade (Section~\ref{sec:quality}), computed when it is written,
and every merged event its lineage (Section~\ref{sec:merge}).

Event types, evaluation modes, and evaluation statuses are stored in the
QuakeML~1.2 BED enumerations (a few common non-BED event types, such as volcanic
tremor, are kept and mapped to BED types on export), so that these fields are
comparable across catalogues.  Magnitude types are stored as reported:
QuakeML defines no controlled vocabulary for them, and their case carries
meaning.

\subsection{Versioning and provenance}

CofC adopts a three-tier versioning convention (\textsc{major.minor.patch})
following semantic-versioning conventions and DataCite data-versioning
best practice \citep{datacite2021}.  The platform manages the version itself:
a catalogue starts at 1.0.0 when first loaded, and each operation on it
releases one new version, with the time of the change recorded, according to
the following policy:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
  \item \textbf{Major} increments when event parameters of existing events
    change (an edited event, or a GeoNet re-import that updates existing
    events).
  \item \textbf{Minor} increments when events are added to a catalogue that
    already held events, without changing existing solutions.
  \item \textbf{Patch} increments for catalogue name or metadata corrections
    that do not affect event parameters.
\end{itemize}
Recomputing derived fields such as the quality score does not release a version.
The version label a depositor gives the source dataset is kept separately.
Every export records the catalogue's version and a checksum of the exported rows
(Section~\ref{sec:export}), so a study can cite the version it used and a reader
can verify a copy against it.  The platform does not keep the event rows of
past versions, so a superseded version cannot be retrieved from it: each major
and minor release is intended to be deposited with an external archive (e.g.\
Zenodo) to obtain a persistent DOI, and DOI minting is not performed by the
platform itself.  Every merged catalogue carries event-level provenance: the
source catalogue IDs, every contributing report (with the one whose solution
was published marked), the merge strategy and threshold parameters applied,
the quality score of the published solution and, for a group held back for
review, its review status.  This per-event provenance is
carried in the CSV, JSON, GeoJSON and KML exports and as a per-event QuakeML
\texttt{comment} \citep{schorlemmer2011}, and merge-level metadata is recorded
at the catalogue level, supporting the reproducibility goals of CSEP-based
forecast evaluations \citep{schorlemmer2007}.

\subsection{Visualisation}
\label{sec:viz}

CofC provides the following interactive views, on each catalogue's map page and
on the Analysis page, where every view reflects the same event filters.

\textbf{Interactive map.}  The map view is built on Leaflet
\citep{leaflet2024} and renders events as circles whose size steps up with
magnitude and whose colour can encode focal depth, quality grade, azimuthal gap,
or source catalogue (one colour per contributing catalogue, a merged event taking
the colour of the catalogue whose solution it published), and on the Analysis
page also magnitude; the legend is generated from the same colouring.  Events are drawn on a
single canvas layer (Leaflet \texttt{preferCanvas}) and, for very large
catalogues, sub-sampled to maintain browser responsiveness.  The sample is
\emph{spatial}: the visible extent is divided into screen cells and the largest
event in each occupied cell is retained as its representative, any remaining
capacity being filled evenly through the catalogue.  The plotted set
is therefore deliberately not proportional to the catalogue's
magnitude--frequency distribution, and neither the symbol density nor the
magnitude mix should be read quantitatively.  On the Analysis page two overlays,
off by default, can be switched on: (i) \emph{uncertainty ellipses}, which draw
each plotted event's horizontal location uncertainty from the agency's reported
error ellipse where there is one, else from its circular horizontal uncertainty,
else from the latitude and longitude uncertainties (a circle of the semi-major
axis when no orientation is reported), stating the ellipse's confidence level
when the source recorded it; and (ii) \emph{focal-mechanism beach balls},
lower-hemisphere diagrams drawn from each event's preferred focal mechanism.
Both act on the plotted events only and are limited to the 150 largest of them,
with a notice when the limit applies.

\textbf{Station coverage.}  Station coverage is shown through the per-event
azimuthal gap, which is the coverage measure the catalogues report (no station
coordinates are stored per event): in the azimuthal-gap colour mode the map
colours events on a continuous ramp from green (small gap) through orange to red
at $180^{\circ}$, continuing into magenta and violet beyond $180^{\circ}$ so that
poorly constrained events stand out, with events that report no gap in grey.  It
shows in map view what a gap distribution such as Figure~\ref{fig:gap}a shows in
aggregate, so that regions where network geometry is insufficient for reliable
locations stand out spatially.

\textbf{Frequency-magnitude distribution.}  The Gutenberg-Richter and $M_c$
tabs plot the cumulative and non-cumulative event counts by magnitude bin and
overlay the fitted GR line and the $M_c$ estimate; under the frequency-magnitude
distribution the $M_c$ tab plots the $b$-value stability test at every cut-off,
whichever method set $M_c$ (Section~\ref{sec:mc}).  When the magnitude filter
has a lower bound, the fit uses it as an explicit cut-off (and says so); the
filter's upper bound is never applied to the fit, which needs the distribution's
upper tail, and $M_c$ itself is always estimated from the untruncated
distribution, with the method and correction chosen in the $M_c$ settings
(Section~\ref{sec:mc}).

\textbf{Time-series panels.}  The Temporal tab shows a seismicity-rate series,
the cumulative event count, magnitude against origin time (with the $M_c$ or
cut-off line; above 3\,000 events a sample made of the largest events plus an
even stride in time), and the cumulative release of seismic moment or radiated
energy.  The rate series counts the events at or above the magnitude cut-off,
or above the estimated $M_c$ when no cut-off is set, per bin of one UTC day, ISO
week or calendar month (by default daily for spans up to a year and weekly
beyond), so that step changes in network completeness show up as rate anomalies.
A first or last bin that the analysed period covers only in part is drawn dashed.
It is scaled to a full bin when that period is known: the time filter's window
and, when a single catalogue is analysed, the catalogue's declared time period,
intersected when both are set (the end is exclusive, and a date-only end covers
that day).  Otherwise its coverage can only be read off the first or last event,
so it is drawn at its raw count, with a note that it may understate the rate.
Cumulative curves are plotted at the end of each bin, and events whose origin
time cannot be parsed are left out of the series and counted.
The Analysis page withholds the moment and energy totals, like the $b$-value and
$M_c$, for a set pooled from several catalogues, which would count shared earthquakes
more than once.

\section{Quality Scoring and Validation}
\label{sec:quality}

CofC computes an event-level quality index on import and makes it available to
every downstream step: merging, filtering, and analysis.  This section defines
that index and the companion cross-field consistency checks.

\subsection{Event-level quality index}

Upon import every event receives a quality score $Q \in [0,100]$ computed as
a weighted sum over five dimensions of solution quality:
\begin{equation}
  Q = w_{\text{loc}} Q_{\text{loc}}
    + w_{\text{net}} Q_{\text{net}}
    + w_{\text{sol}} Q_{\text{sol}}
    + w_{\text{mag}} Q_{\text{mag}}
    + w_{\text{eval}} Q_{\text{eval}},
  \label{eq:quality}
\end{equation}
with default weights $w_{\text{loc}} = 0.35$, $w_{\text{net}} = 0.25$,
$w_{\text{sol}} = 0.15$, $w_{\text{mag}} = 0.15$, $w_{\text{eval}} = 0.10$.
The weights are largest for the dimensions that most directly govern whether a
solution is usable in quantitative analysis: location precision and network
geometry are the primary determinants of hypocentral accuracy
\citep{bondar2004,warrensmith2025}; the arrival-time fit and magnitude quality
are secondary; and the analyst-evaluation status, informative but partly
redundant with the instrumental metrics, carries the least weight.  Each
dimension applies an explicit penalty when its underlying fields are absent,
so events with sparse metadata are scored conservatively.  The weights are
intentionally round-valued and are exposed as configurable parameters of the
scoring module (defaults above), so that communities with different priorities
can adjust them without modifying the analysis code.  Because $Q_{\text{loc}}$
and $Q_{\text{net}}$ are functions of network density, $Q$ is positively
correlated with network coverage by construction: it is an explicit,
reproducible ranking rather than a network-neutral measure, and the default
weights are a design choice not yet calibrated against an independent
ground-truth benchmark (Section~\ref{sec:discussion}).  Field completeness,
physical-consistency checks, and magnitude-scale homogeneity are handled
separately by the cross-field validation suite (Section~\ref{sec:crossfield}).
The five dimension scores $Q_d \in [0,100]$ are:

\begin{description}[leftmargin=2em]
  \item[$Q_{\text{loc}}$: Location (35\%)]  Solution precision from the
    reported horizontal, depth, and time uncertainties (up to 40, 30 and 30
    points of penalty, reached at 10~km, 10~km and 1~s).  A missing uncertainty
    takes the penalty of the worst reportable value, so an unconstrained
    solution cannot score full marks.
  \item[$Q_{\text{net}}$: Network geometry (25\%)]  Azimuthal gap,
    used-station count, and used-phase count (up to 50, 30 and 20 points of
    penalty).  Azimuthal gap is among the principal predictors of horizontal
    location error \citep{bondar2004,warrensmith2025}: a gap below $90^{\circ}$
    and a well-populated network ($\geq 20$ stations) each earn full marks on
    their respective (additive) terms, whereas a gap of $180^{\circ}$ loses 45
    points and few stations up to 30.  Because $w_{\text{net}} = 0.25$, the gap
    term on its own can lower $Q$ by at most 12.5 points; events with large gaps
    score low overall when, as usual, they also carry large location
    uncertainties and few stations.  These cut-offs are CofC design heuristics;
    GeoNet location quality has been assessed against azimuthal-gap and
    station-coverage metrics of this kind \citep{warrensmith2025}.
  \item[$Q_{\text{sol}}$: Solution fit (15\%)]  The RMS arrival-time residual
    (standard error) of the location: $< 0.3$\,s earns full points, with
    progressively larger penalties above $0.5$ and $1.0$\,s.
  \item[$Q_{\text{mag}}$: Magnitude (15\%)]  Magnitude uncertainty and the
    number of stations contributing to the magnitude; small uncertainties and
    $\geq 10$ amplitude measurements earn full points.
  \item[$Q_{\text{eval}}$: Evaluation (10\%)]  Evaluation mode and status:
    manually reviewed or final solutions score highest; automatic or
    preliminary solutions are penalised, reflecting the additional confidence
    conferred by analyst review, and a solution its agency rejected earns
    nothing on this dimension.
\end{description}
Values outside a field's physical range (for example a $-999$ missing-value
sentinel) are treated as absent, and $Q$ and every dimension score are clamped
to $[0,100]$.  The score is stored with every event when it is written, including
merged events, whose $Q$ is that of the solution they publish.

Figure~\ref{fig:quality} summarises the five dimensions, their weights, and the
grade mapping; the score $Q$ maps to the letter grades in
Table~\ref{tab:grades}.  Grades are displayed per event on the map and table

\subsection{Cross-field validation}
\label{sec:crossfield}

CofC applies a suite of physical-consistency checks at upload time and
presents a summary before the user commits to saving:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
  \item Magnitude-depth plausibility (e.g., $M > 8$ at depth $< \SI{5}{\km}$
    is extremely rare).
  \item Depth uncertainty exceeding twice the depth value.
  \item Magnitude uncertainty exceeding the magnitude for $|M| \geq 1$
    (physically implausible).
  \item Azimuthal gap $> 180^{\circ}$ (the recording stations span less than
    half the azimuths around the event), or a gap below the $360^{\circ}/N$
    minimum that $N$ stations can achieve.
  \item Used-station count exceeding the used-phase count.
  \item RMS travel-time residual $< \SI{0.001}{\s}$ (suspiciously small, may
    indicate synthetic or processed data).
  \item Location-uncertainty anisotropy ratio $> 10$ (strongly elongated error
    ellipse suggesting poor azimuthal coverage or geometry).
\end{itemize}
Each check reports a severity level (\textit{error}, \textit{warning},
\textit{info}) and a remediation suggestion.  Checks of all three severities are
surfaced to the user
before saving; the checks are advisory and currently annotate rather than reject
the affected event, so a record flagged \textit{error} is still ingested and
carries its flag.

\section{Catalogue Merging}
\label{sec:merge}

\subsection{Duplicate detection}

Duplicate detection uses an adaptive time-and-distance matching window.  Two
events from different source catalogues are considered duplicate candidates when
both
\begin{align}
  |\Delta t|     &\leq \tau, \label{eq:dt} \\
  d              &\leq \delta, \label{eq:dd}
\end{align}
where $d$ is the great-circle distance computed from coordinates and the baseline
windows $\tau$ and $\delta$, which the user sets (the merge page starts at
60\,s and 10\,km; the user guide suggests 60\,s and 50\,km for a national
catalogue), are widened automatically by fixed magnitude-dependent multipliers.  In time the multiplier is
$1.0/1.5/2.0/3.0$ for $M<4$, $4\leq M<5.5$, $5.5\leq M<7$, and $M\geq7$
respectively; in distance it is $1.0/1.5/2.5/4.0$ over the same bands, with an
additional depth factor of $1.0/1.2/1.5$ for depths $<100$, $100$--$300$, and
$>300$\,km applied to the distance window.  Association uses the great-circle
\emph{epicentral} distance; depth enters only through this widening factor,
reflecting the poorer depth control of sparse networks.  Magnitude is not part
of the matching test; magnitude consistency is instead enforced as the per-group
range gate described below.  Near-antimeridian pairs use a great-circle distance
and unit-vector coordinate averaging to avoid sign-wrapping errors in the
Pacific region.

The baseline windows are user-configurable in the merge UI and are consistent in
scale with the $10$--$20$\,km epicentral scatter documented for multi-network
locations \citep{engdahl1998}; configurable space--time association windows of
this kind are also provided by catalogue-building toolkits \citep{oqmbtk}.
Association is one to one and best match first.  Every pair of reports from
\emph{different} catalogues that fall inside each other's widened windows is a
candidate, ranked by its normalised separation $|\Delta t|/\tau + d/\delta$ (so
time and distance count equally whatever the window sizes; magnitude agreement
only breaks ties).  Candidates are joined in increasing separation, so each report
is claimed by its closest counterpart in each other catalogue rather than by
whichever earlier report reached it first; a group never holds two reports from
one catalogue (each catalogue is taken to be free of internal duplicates, and a
merge request that lists the same catalogue twice is refused), and a group of
three or more must have a member inside the window of every other
member, so reports linked only through a chain are not fused.  A group that fails
the validity gates below is split into its largest self-consistent sub-groups
(seeded from its earliest report, candidates tried nearest first), and reports left
unpaired may pair again in a further round.  The result does not depend on the
order of the input.  The merge preview flags an \emph{ambiguous association}
when a report's best refused alternative was nearly as close as the pairing kept
(within twice its separation plus a tenth of a window), since time and distance
alone then barely separate the two choices.

Candidate groups that violate magnitude-scaled physical-validity gates are
flagged as merge conflicts and are not merged.  A group is rejected if its
magnitude range exceeds $0.5$ ($M<4$), $0.8$ ($M\,4$--$5.5$), $1.2$
($M\,5.5$--$7$), or $1.5$ ($M\geq7$) within any one magnitude scale, or, for a
group mixing scales, on the common $M_w$ scale with the threshold widened by the
conversion uncertainties (on the raw values when a type cannot be converted); if
its depth range exceeds $30$\,km for shallow events
($50$\,km from $M5$), with wider limits at intermediate and great depth; if a
group of more than three spreads over more than $100$\,km ($M<5$), $150$\,km
($M\,5$--$6$), or $200$\,km ($M\geq6$); or if more than $15$ events fall in a
single group.

Whatever the strategy, the published origin keeps its own metadata (agency,
method, gap, counts, residual, evaluation status) and the published depth its own
type and uncertainty: none of these is filled in from another report, and a
location or magnitude uncertainty is filled in only from a report stating the
same location, or the same magnitude value and type.  The Average values strategy
selects the magnitude rather than averaging it, and publishes it as reported, not
converted.  The preference depends on the event's size, taken as the median of the
reported magnitudes on the $M_w$ scale ($M_L$ as $M_w$; $m_b$ and $M_s$ by the
relations of \citealp{scordilis2006}).  $M_w$ comes first, with proxy $M_w$ types
($M_w(m_B)$, $M_{wp}$) below true ones.  Below $M6.2$, the $M_L$ family (including
GeoNet's summary magnitude $M$) comes before $m_b$, $m_B$ and $m_{bLg}$, then
$M_s$, then $M_d$; from $M6.2$, $M_s$ comes first, then $m_B$, $M_L$, $m_b$ and
$m_{bLg}$, and $M_d$.  The switch is at 6.2 because there the $M_s$ relation
becomes one to one while $M_L$ still reads $M_w$, so the published value stays
within about 0.1 of $M_w$ on either side.  Rejected magnitudes are skipped; ties go
to the smaller uncertainty, then to the agency's preferred magnitude.

Three refinements apply on top of any strategy.  First, the depth, magnitude
and focal mechanism of the merged event may each be resolved by a rule of their
own rather than by the strategy: the depth from the best-constrained report, or
from the report ranked first by quality, network authority or determination
time, or from a named catalogue; the magnitude by the type preference above or
from a report chosen in the same ways; the focal mechanism from the united set
(the default), from the published report only, or from a named catalogue.  The
published depth and magnitude always carry their own metadata, and the report
that supplied each is marked in the lineage.  Second, a group the preview flags
(a failed validity gate, a split group, an ambiguous pairing, or an inconsistent
magnitude or depth range) may be held for review instead of resolved: the
strategy's solution is written as provisional, the event is listed on the
catalogue page with the reasons, and an editor either keeps that solution or
publishes one contributing report wholesale, which bumps the catalogue version.
Third, two reports of one agency in a group (the agency of their catalogue, not
merely the author of a copied origin) are compared on their agency event
identifiers, reduced to a bare form: equal identifiers are vintages of one
solution, of which only the most recently computed takes part, the older being
kept in the lineage as superseded; differing identifiers are two earthquakes,
and the group is split, each report left alone being flagged with the reason;
without comparable identifiers both reports take part as usual.  The network-authority table that the Source priority fallback,
the Quality-based tie-break and the authority rule consult (a global ranking
with regional overrides for New Zealand and Japan by default) is editable by an
administrator, and each merged event records which table was used.

The Quality-based strategy is recommended because it selects the
best-constrained solution by an explicit, configurable rule rather than a
subjective ranking of agencies, and it preserves all provenance information.
Because several quality sub-scores increase with network density
(Section~\ref{sec:quality}), this strategy systematically favours solutions from
denser networks; it is a reproducible, explicit preference, not a network-neutral
one, and we recommend inspecting the per-strategy provenance split
(Section~\ref{sec:example}) as a bias diagnostic.  Focal mechanisms from all
reports in a group are united, and the preferred one is chosen by source (Global
CMT, then USGS/NEIC, GEOFON, GeoNet and INGV moment tensors, then other moment
tensors, then first-motion solutions from at least 20 polarities, then automatic
ones) and within a source by the quality of the solution.

\section{Statistical Analyses}
\label{sec:analysis}

\subsection{Gutenberg-Richter $b$-value}

The Gutenberg-Richter (GR) frequency-magnitude relation \citep{gutenberg1944}
is
\begin{equation}
  \log_{10} N(M) = a - bM,
  \label{eq:GR}
\end{equation}
where $N(M)$ is the cumulative number of earthquakes with magnitude $\geq M$,
$a$ is the $\log_{10}$ event-rate intercept, and $b \approx 1$ for tectonic
seismicity \citep{gutenberg1944}.

CofC estimates $b$ using the maximum-likelihood estimator of
\citet{aki1965,utsu1965}:
\begin{equation}
  \hat{b} = \frac{\log_{10} e}{\bar{M} - M_{\min} + \Delta M / 2},
  \label{eq:aki}
\end{equation}
where $\bar{M}$ is the sample mean magnitude, $M_{\min}$ is the lower
magnitude cut-off (the estimated $M_c$, or an explicit cut-off; see
Section~\ref{sec:mc}), and $\Delta M$ is the step to which the magnitudes are
\emph{reported}, not the width of the plotted histogram bins
\citep{bender1983}: a magnitude rounded to $0.1$ stands for an interval
$0.1$ wide, so the correction $\Delta M/2$ is $0.05$ for magnitudes reported to
$0.1$, $0.005$ for $0.01$, and $0$ for magnitudes reported to full precision (as
GeoNet reports them).  CofC detects the reporting step from the magnitudes
themselves, counting a magnitude within $2^{-20}$ of a grid value as lying on
it: the steps $0.1$, $0.05$, $0.01$ and $0.001$, and the coarse steps $0.2$,
$0.25$ and $0.5$ of historical and intensity-derived magnitudes when at least
95\% of the sample lies on one of them.  For a catalogue that mixes steps, such
as a merged one, it applies the correction weighted by the share of the sample
at each step, and it reports the step and the correction with each estimate.
For the coarse steps the $\Delta M/2$ correction is itself approximate and leaves
$\hat{b}$ 2--10\% low at $b \approx 1$; the binned estimator of
\citet{bender1983} is exact.  Equation~\eqref{eq:aki} is the
uncorrected maximum-likelihood estimator and is biased high for small samples
($\mathrm{E}[\hat{b}] = b\,N/(N-1)$; \citealp{marzocchi2003}), so at the minimum
fitting count above an explicit cut-off ($N=10$; Section~\ref{sec:mc}) $\hat{b}$ is
overestimated by $\sim$11\%; we therefore recommend $N \gtrsim 200$ for reported
$b$-values.  The
standard error on $\hat{b}$ is \citep{aki1965}
\begin{equation}
  \sigma_b = \frac{\hat{b}}{\sqrt{N}}.
  \label{eq:sigma_b}
\end{equation}
Equation~\eqref{eq:sigma_b} is the asymptotic counting-statistics error and
\emph{understates} the true uncertainty: it ignores the finite-sample variance
of the observed magnitudes captured by the \citet{shibolt1982} estimator and the
magnitude-binning correction of \citet{bender1983}, and it excludes the
systematic sensitivity of $\hat{b}$ to the choice of $M_c$, to magnitude-scale
heterogeneity, and to declustering, which usually dominate the total
uncertainty (Section~\ref{sec:biases}).  We therefore treat the
formal $\sigma_b$ of Equation~\eqref{eq:sigma_b} as a lower bound and caution that
realistic uncertainties are larger.  CofC computes $\sigma_b = \hat{b}/\sqrt{N}$
and returns it with each Gutenberg-Richter estimate, displayed alongside the GR
plot (cumulative and non-cumulative) and the fitted line.

\subsection{Magnitude of completeness}
\label{sec:mc}

The magnitude of completeness $M_c$ is estimated by one of three methods, chosen
in the $M_c$ settings of the Analysis page.  The default is the $b$-value
stability method (MBS) of \citet{caogao2002}, in the form of
\citet{woessner2005}.  Each bin edge $M_i$, in steps of $0.1$, with at least 50
events at or above it in at least three populated bins is a candidate cut-off;
$b(M_i)$ is estimated from the $n$ events at or above it by
equation~\eqref{eq:aki} (with the reporting-step correction), with the
uncertainty of \citet{shibolt1982},
\begin{equation}
  \delta b(M_i) = 2.3\,b^2(M_i)
    \sqrt{\frac{\sum_{k=1}^{n} (M_k - \bar{M})^2}{n(n-1)}},
  \label{eq:shibolt}
\end{equation}
and $\bar{b}(M_i)$ is the mean of $b$ over the six cut-offs $M_i$, $M_i + 0.1$,
\ldots, $M_i + 0.5$, every one of them a candidate.  $M_c$ is the lowest $M_i$ with
\begin{equation}
  \left|\bar{b}(M_i) - b(M_i)\right| \leq \delta b(M_i),
  \label{eq:mbs}
\end{equation}
so the test needs at least 50 events at or above $M_i + 0.5$, some 160 at or
above $M_i$ for $b = 1$.  If no candidate passes, CofC reports the
goodness-of-fit estimate below, or failing that the MAXC estimate, and says so.
MBS is conservative by construction: it accepts a cut-off only where $\hat{b}$
has stopped changing, within its own uncertainty, over the next half magnitude
unit.  Because $\delta b \approx \hat{b}/\sqrt{n}$, about $0.005$ for 40\,000
events at $b = 1$, on a large catalogue it also rejects cut-offs at which
$\hat{b}$ still drifts by only a few hundredths, whether from residual
incompleteness, a mixture of populations with different $b$-values, or an
excess of large events over the GR law, and $M_c$ rises accordingly: on the
worked example's catalogues of about $10^5$ events it lies 0.7--0.9 units above
the goodness-of-fit estimate (Section~\ref{sec:example}).  Whichever method is
selected, the $M_c$ tab therefore plots $b(M_i) \pm \delta b(M_i)$ and
$\bar{b}(M_i)$ against the cut-off, with $M_c$ marked, so that a cut-off raised by
a drift the analyst judges immaterial can be recognised and replaced by an
explicit one.

The first alternative is the Goodness-of-Fit Test (GFT) of \citet{wiemer2000},
in the form of \citet{woessner2005}.  Each bin edge $M_i$ with
at least 10 events at or above it in at least three populated bins is a
candidate cut-off; $b$ is estimated above it by equation~\eqref{eq:aki} (with the
same reporting-step correction), the predicted cumulative counts
$S_j = N(\geq M_i)\,10^{-b(M_j - M_i)}$ are compared with the observed counts
$B_j = N(\geq M_j)$ at every bin edge $M_j \geq M_i$ up to the bin holding the
largest magnitude, and the fit is
$R = 100 - 100\sum_j |B_j - S_j| / \sum_j B_j$.  $M_c$ is the lowest candidate
with $R \geq 95\%$, else the lowest with $R \geq 90\%$; if no candidate reaches
$90\%$, CofC reports the MAXC estimate and says so.  The Analysis page plots $R$
against the candidate cut-off, with the chosen $M_c$ marked.  The second
alternative is the Maximum Curvature (MAXC) method \citep{wiemer2000}:
\begin{equation}
  M_c^{\mathrm{MAXC}} = \operatorname*{arg\,max}_{M}
    \; N_{\mathrm{non\text{-}cum}}(M) + c,
  \label{eq:maxc}
\end{equation}
i.e.\ the centre of the $0.1$-wide magnitude bin containing the most events in
the non-cumulative frequency-magnitude distribution (the lowest such bin on a tie),
plus a correction $c$.  As in \citet{wiemer2000}, each magnitude is binned to its
nearest multiple of $0.1$, so the bin labelled $M$ holds $[M - 0.05, M + 0.05)$
and the $M_c$ tab plots the same bins; for magnitudes reported to $0.1$ this is
the reported value, whereas labelling the bins by their lower edges would bias
the estimate for full-precision magnitudes low by half a bin on average.  MAXC is
fast and makes no assumption about the GR model, but tends to underestimate $M_c$
by $\sim 0.1$--$0.2$ magnitude units for typical networks \citep{woessner2005};
CofC therefore adds $c = 0.2$ by default (also when MBS or the GFT falls back to
MAXC), which the user can set from $0$ to $0.5$ in steps of $0.1$, and every
result states the correction it used.

The formal uncertainty on $M_c$ is at least $\pm 0.1$ (one bin width), but
\citet{woessner2005} showed empirically that the true uncertainty is typically
$0.2$--$0.3$ magnitude units, for MAXC because the non-cumulative FMD peak is
broad and sensitive to sample fluctuations.  CofC displays $M_c$ with the bin
width as a lower bound on its uncertainty and recommends visual verification
against the FMD and $b$-value stability plots.  All three methods estimate a
single $M_c$ for the events under analysis; where completeness varies in space or
time, as in most merged catalogues, the MAXC and goodness-of-fit values in
particular can lie well below the completeness of parts of the catalogue
(Section~\ref{sec:example}).  The Entire Magnitude Range method
\citep{woessner2005} remains external post-processing.

A commonly adopted minimum for a reliable $b$-value estimate is $N \approx 200$
events above $M_c$ (cf.\ \citealt{marzocchi2003}); below this the formal
$\sigma_b = \hat{b}/\sqrt{N}$ exceeds $\approx 0.07$ (for $\hat{b} \approx 1$),
comparable to the biases
discussed in Section~\ref{sec:biases}, and the confidence interval on $b$ becomes
too wide for quantitative hazard use.  CofC enforces hard floors below which an
estimate is withheld rather than reported: estimating $M_c$ requires at least 50
events; a Gutenberg-Richter fit requires at least 10 events at or above its
cut-off, spread over at least three populated magnitude bins (and, when the fit
estimates its own $M_c$, the 50 events above); and the $b$-value of an individual
aftershock sequence is reported only for sequences of at least 50 events, above
the sequence's own $M_c$ (by $b$-value stability, with its fallbacks), since
short-term incompleteness early in a sequence
raises its $M_c$ above the background network's.  The $N \approx 200$ figure is a
guideline the analyst should apply when interpreting results.

\subsection{Declustering}
\label{sec:decluster}

Aftershock sequences violate the Poissonian independence assumption underlying
GR analysis.  CofC implements the Gardner-Knopoff window method
\citep{gardner1974} with the commonly tabulated magnitude-dependent windows
(as compiled by \citealt{vanstiphout2012}).  For each event the space-time
interaction zone is:
\begin{align}
  L(M)  &= 10^{0.1238M + 0.983}\;\text{km}, \label{eq:gk_L}\\
  T(M)  &= \begin{cases}
            10^{0.032M + 2.7389}\;\text{days}, & M \geq 6.5, \\
            10^{0.5409M - 0.547}\;\text{days}, & M < 6.5
           \end{cases}, \label{eq:gk_T}
\end{align}
the two-branch time window $T(M)$ following the larger-magnitude relation for
$M \geq 6.5$ and the small-magnitude relation otherwise.  Events are visited in
order of decreasing magnitude, and every not-yet-assigned event within $L(M)$ and
$T(M)$ of the visited event is assigned to its cluster; each visited event is
itself reserved, so a later, smaller event cannot absorb a larger one.  CofC
applies $T(M)$ \emph{forward} in time from the mainshock, as in the original
\citet{gardner1974} procedure and as the OpenQuake \texttt{hmtk} reference
implementation does with its foreshock-window proportion
(\texttt{fs\_time\_prop}) set to zero; foreshocks are therefore not removed.  (A
symmetric window that also reaches back $T(M)$ before each mainshock is a
common variant; on the synthetic catalogue of Section~\ref{sec:example} it
removes a further 970 events above the analysis cut-off and lowers the
declustered $\hat{b}$ by $0.06$, about four formal standard errors, so the
choice is not cosmetic and is stated here.)  The Reasenberg
\citeyearpar{reasenberg1985} algorithm, which uses an interaction zone based on
the Omori--Utsu law \citep{omori1894,utsu1961} and adapts to the local
seismicity rate, is implemented in the analysis library as an alternative,
though the browser worker currently exposes only the Gardner-Knopoff method.

Declustering returns a mainshock/aftershock partition for the events under
analysis; an event whose origin time cannot be parsed falls in no window and is
left independent, and the Temporal tab reports how many such events there are.
Exports can carry it: with the export option for Gardner-Knopoff
tags, each exported event gains its cluster (identified by its mainshock) and a
mainshock flag, and the export records the algorithm, the windows used and the
counts; writing the partition back as a separate catalogue within the platform
is not implemented.  Events preceding a larger mainshock are retained; the
declustered set is the mainshock population in the usual window-method sense
\citep{vanstiphout2012}.

\subsection{Cumulative seismic moment}

Cumulative seismic moment is computed as
\begin{equation}
  M_0 = 10^{1.5 M_w + 9.1}\;\text{N\,m},
  \label{eq:M0}
\end{equation}
using the Hanks-Kanamori relation \citep{hanks1979,kanamori1977} in SI units
(equivalently $M_w = \tfrac{2}{3}\log_{10} M_0 - 6.07$ for $M_0$ in
\si{\newton\metre}).  The sum applies Equation~\eqref{eq:M0} to $M_w$ as
reported and, assuming $M_L \approx M_w$, to $M_L$, to GeoNet's summary
magnitude $M$ and to untyped magnitudes; $m_b$, $m_B$, $M_s$, $M_d$ and other stated types are excluded, and
the Analysis page states how many events were treated each way.  (The library's
empirical conversions, after \citet{scordilis2006} for $m_b$ and $M_s$, serve the
merge's magnitude-consistency gate and its estimate of an event's size for the
magnitude-type preference; automatic homogenisation of mixed magnitude
types within the analysis pipeline remains future work,
Section~\ref{sec:biases}.)  Because cumulative moment is dominated by the largest
events, magnitude saturation (Section~\ref{sec:biases}) in any saturated input
magnitudes propagates strongly to $\sum M_0$; $\sum M_0$ is therefore only
physically meaningful when the input magnitudes are moment magnitudes or close
proxies for them, and totals that rely on the $M_L \approx M_w$ assumption should
be treated as indicative.  The Temporal tab plots the cumulative release of
seismic moment, or of radiated energy ($\log_{10} E = 1.5M + 4.8$, in joules;
for $M_w$ this is $E = M_0/(2 \times 10^{4})$, \citealp{kanamori1977}), against
time for the same eligible events.

\subsection{Temporal pattern analysis}

Beyond the aggregate GR analysis, CofC provides two temporal diagnostics
for detecting non-stationarity in the catalogue.

\textbf{Seismicity rate time series.}  Events at or above a magnitude threshold
are counted per time bin (one UTC day, ISO week or calendar month, chosen by the
user, or by default daily for spans up to a year and weekly beyond) and plotted as
a rate time series.  The threshold is the magnitude filter's lower bound when one
is set, and otherwise the $M_c$ estimated under the current $M_c$ settings; with
fewer than 50 events, too few to estimate $M_c$, every event is counted and the
page says that the rate then also follows changes in detection.  Partial first
and last bins are scaled or marked as described in Section~\ref{sec:viz}, and
events whose origin time cannot be parsed are left out of the series and
counted.  Sudden rate changes, which often correspond to network upgrades, station outages, or changes
in processing procedures, are immediately visible and can be used to identify
breakpoints for time-varying $M_c$ analysis \citep{mignan2012}.

\textbf{Interevent-time distribution} (specified, not yet implemented)\textbf{.}
  The distribution of times between successive events above $M_c$ is to be
plotted against the exponential distribution expected for a Poissonian process.  Systematic departures, most commonly the
excess of short interevent times produced by aftershock clusters, provide a
visual diagnostic of whether the catalogue has been adequately declustered
before analyses that assume Poissonian seismicity.  The exponential reference
assumes a stationary rate; for strongly non-stationary catalogues the time-rescaling
(residual) approach of \citet{ogata1988} should be applied before interpreting
departures as clustering.

\section{Export Formats}
\label{sec:export}

Processed or merged catalogues can be exported in:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
  \item \textbf{CSV}: a flat table, one row per event, with the stored fields
    and per-event lineage columns (source-catalogue IDs, merge strategy and
    parameters, the selected source report and its catalogue, quality score and
    grade, review status, the agency's own event type, and the catalogue
    version), suitable for
    ZMAP \citep{wiemer2001}, ObsPy \citep{obspy2010}, and R/Python workflows;
    an optional commented prologue carries the catalogue metadata.
  \item \textbf{QuakeML~1.2 BED} \citep{schorlemmer2011}: re-serialisation
    of the stored origins, magnitudes, and extended fields (a merged event's
    published solution is either the contributing agency's own origin or a new
    origin identified as merged), with each event's lineage as an event-level
    \texttt{comment}, catalogue-level merge metadata as \texttt{comment}
    elements, and the catalogue version in the creation information; the output
    validates against the QuakeML-BED~1.2 schema.
  \item \textbf{JSON} and \textbf{GeoJSON} \citep{geojson2016}: the same
    per-event fields and lineage as properties, with the merge configuration and
    source catalogues in the document head; GeoJSON suits GIS software and web
    mapping.
  \item \textbf{KML}: Open Geospatial Consortium Keyhole Markup Language,
    for Google Earth and similar viewers, with magnitude-scaled symbols.
\end{itemize}
Exports may be taken for a whole catalogue or for the events matching the
catalogue page's filters (magnitude, depth and time ranges, a bounding box that
may cross the antimeridian, event and magnitude types, evaluation status and
mode, azimuthal gap, station and phase counts, RMS residual, per-field
uncertainty limits, and a minimum quality score).  Every export records the
catalogue's identifier and version (with the time that version was released),
the export time (UTC), the filter and any declustering applied (Gardner-Knopoff
tags are an option; Section~\ref{sec:decluster}), and a SHA-256 checksum over the
exported event rows that is identical in every format for the same selection;
the version also appears in the file name.  A study can therefore cite the
version it used and check a copy against it; because the platform does not keep
superseded versions, the export itself, or an archive deposit of it, is the record
of that data state (Section~\ref{sec:architecture}).

\section{Worked Example: New Zealand Seismicity}
\label{sec:example}

To illustrate the CofC workflow and quantify the statistical biases described
in Section~\ref{sec:biases}, we construct a \emph{synthetic} catalogue pair
representative of two agencies monitoring New Zealand seismicity and process it
with the platform's own engine.  The construction is a seeded model, not a fit to
any real dataset; its parameters are listed in the electronic supplement
(Table~S1).  One population of true earthquakes spans January 2020--December
2024: an independent background with a Gutenberg-Richter $b = 1.00$ spread over
seven New Zealand source zones, at about 3\,200 events of $M \geq 2.3$ per year,
plus aftershock sequences with $b = 1.30$ injected on the background events of
$M \geq 4.5$ (Omori-law timing, \citealp{omori1894,utsu1961}), sized so that 23\%
of the true events of $M \geq 2.0$ are aftershocks.  Two synthetic station
networks record them: a GeoNet-like national network of 166 stations at
$\sim$40~km spacing on land, and an Agency~B regional network of 67 stations at
$\sim$30~km spacing in the central and eastern North Island
(Figure~\ref{fig:map}a).  An agency catalogues every earthquake that four or more
of its stations record within a magnitude-dependent distance.  Each report's
azimuthal gap, station count and nearest-station distance follow from its
network's geometry; its location, depth, origin-time and magnitude uncertainties
follow stated scalings with those quantities (growing with the gap and with the
distance outside the network, falling with the number of stations); and its
reported values are the true ones perturbed by errors of that size.  Both agencies
report analyst-reviewed $M_L$, the GeoNet-like agency to full precision and
Agency~B to 0.1.  The resulting catalogues hold 113\,427 (GeoNet-like) and
101\,806 (Agency~B) events, of the order of the GeoNet national catalogue over the
same period.  Every number below is computed from these catalogues by the
platform's own modules, called as the platform calls them (Data and Resources);
the generator's ground truth is used only to score the results.

\subsection{Step 1: Import and quality scoring}

The catalogues are loaded with the fields an uploaded QuakeML file stores, and
every event is scored with equation~\eqref{eq:quality} on import.  The median is
$Q = 69$ (grade B) for both catalogues.  The azimuthal gap
(Figure~\ref{fig:gap}a) exceeds $180^{\circ}$ for 25\% of the GeoNet-like and 29\%
of the Agency~B events: the offshore events and, for Agency~B, those outside its
regional network.  Equation~\eqref{eq:quality} ranks these events lowest
(Figure~\ref{fig:gap}b): the median $Q$ falls from 87 for gaps below $90^{\circ}$
to 53--55 between $180^{\circ}$ and $270^{\circ}$ and 35--36 above.  At most 12.5
of those points come from the gap term itself; the rest come from the larger
location uncertainties and smaller station and phase counts that the high-gap
reports carry, a correlation built into the synthetic uncertainty model and one
that real network locations also show \citep{bondar2004}.  The platform's MAXC
estimates of completeness are $M_c = 1.8$ for the GeoNet-like catalogue and 1.4
for Agency~B, whose denser network detects smaller events in its region (the
goodness-of-fit test gives 1.9 for both).

\subsection{Step 2: Merge}

Merging with the Quality-based strategy and baseline windows of 60~s and 50~km
(equations~\eqref{eq:dt} and~\eqref{eq:dd}), the matcher forms 44\,856 duplicate
groups, and the $113\,427 + 101\,806 = 215\,233$ records merge into 170\,377
events (Figure~\ref{fig:map}b): 68\,571 reported by the GeoNet-like agency only,
56\,950 by Agency~B only (mostly small events inside its denser network), and
44\,856 by both.  Because the generator knows which reports describe the same
earthquake, the association can be scored exactly.  Of the 47\,109 earthquakes
both agencies report, 43\,936 (93\%) are paired correctly, and 97.9\% of the
groups join two reports of one earthquake; 915 of the 920 wrong pairings lie
within aftershock sequences, where several events fall inside one window.  Of the
3\,173 pairs left apart, the validity gates reject 2\,315 whose two magnitudes
differ by more than the gate allows (0.5 units below $M4$) and 691 whose depths
differ by more than it allows (30~km for shallow events), most of them because one
agency fixed the depth; 141 lie outside the matching window (distant events
Agency~B locates poorly), and in 26 each report has a closer counterpart.  These earthquakes stay in the merged catalogue twice.
The Quality-based strategy keeps Agency~B's solution for 30\,467 of the duplicate
groups (68\%), those inside its denser network, and the GeoNet-like solution for
the other 14\,389; every contributing report is kept in the merged event's
lineage, and each exported event carries its source catalogue IDs, the merge
strategy, the selected report and its quality score.  These recall and precision
figures reflect the synthetic location, timing and magnitude scatter; they are a
check of the association logic, not a benchmark for real catalogue pairs, whose
errors are non-Gaussian and whose clocks and magnitude scales differ
(Section~\ref{sec:discussion}).

\subsection{Step 3: Quality filtering}

A quality threshold of $Q \geq 70$ (upper grade~B and above) retains 86\,468 of
the 170\,377 merged events (50.8\%).  It removes 94.5\% of the 39\,160 merged
events with gap $> 180^{\circ}$, which make up 44\% of the removed set, so the
filter does remove most of the poorly constrained offshore population.  It is not
neutral in magnitude, however.  Because $Q$ rises with the number of recording
stations and falls with the location and magnitude uncertainties, all of which
depend on magnitude, the filter keeps 58\% of the events of $M$1.5--2.0 but 83\%
of those of $M$3.5--4.0, and so changes the shape of the frequency-magnitude
distribution (Figure~\ref{fig:fmd}a).

\subsection{Step 4: Completeness and $b$-value}

On the quality-filtered catalogue the platform's default estimate is
$M_c = 1.7$ (MAXC with the $+0.2$ correction), above which the maximum-likelihood
fit gives $\hat{b} = 0.811 \pm 0.004$ ($N = 43\,329$); the goodness-of-fit test
gives $M_c = 1.9$ at the 95\% level and $\hat{b} = 0.865 \pm 0.005$.  Both lie
far below the planted values, because completeness varies across the merged
catalogue: Agency~B's network detects small events in the central North Island
that the GeoNet-like network misses, and both networks miss small offshore and
deep events.  A single global $M_c$ is set by the best-covered part of the
catalogue, and the catalogue above it is incomplete elsewhere.  Stepping the
magnitude cut-off shows $\hat{b}$ rising until $M$2.7 and stable above it
(Figure~\ref{fig:fmd}c); $M$2.7 is the lowest cut-off meeting the
$b$-value-stability criterion of \citet{caogao2002}, in the form of
\citet{woessner2005}, applied to the platform's estimates.  Above $M$2.7 the
merged catalogue gives $\hat{b} = 1.061 \pm 0.011$ ($N = 8\,556$), a mixture of
the independent background and the injected aftershocks.  Applied to the
background events alone, the same routine returns $\hat{b} = 1.011 \pm 0.012$
against the planted $1.00$, and to the injected aftershocks alone
$1.35 \pm 0.04$ against the planted $1.30$: the estimator is sound once the
sample is complete.  The quality-filtered catalogue above $M$2.7, in which 15\%
of the events are injected aftershocks, gives $\hat{b} = 0.998 \pm 0.012$
($N = 6\,465$; Figure~\ref{fig:fmd}b), 0.06 below the unfiltered value at the
same cut-off, and its background events alone give $0.955 \pm 0.013$: the
magnitude selectivity of the filter persists above the completeness magnitude.
These are checks of the platform's behaviour on a controlled input, not
measurements of New Zealand seismicity.

\subsection{Step 5: Declustering}

Gardner-Knopoff declustering (Section~\ref{sec:decluster}) of the 6\,465
quality-filtered events above $M$2.7 removes 2\,538 of them (39\%, against the
15\% injected as aftershocks).  The windows recover 96.5\% of the injected
aftershocks but also sweep up 1\,617 independent events that fall inside the
space-time window of a larger event, and these are preferentially small (their
own $\hat{b}$ is 1.19), because every cluster head survives while small events
are the ones absorbed.  The declustered estimate, $\hat{b} = 0.883 \pm 0.014$,
therefore lies 0.12 below both the undeclustered value and the planted background
$b = 1.00$: window declustering shifts $b$ downwards, as \citet{mizrahi2021}
found for real catalogues, and the shift here is not the removal of an
aftershock excess alone.  Because the synthetic aftershocks follow their
mainshocks, the forward window matches this construction; the symmetric window
convention removes a further 970 events and gives $\hat{b} = 0.823 \pm 0.015$,
0.06 (about four formal standard errors) lower still.  Figure~\ref{fig:funnel}
summarises the event-count flow through these steps.

\section{Guidance on Avoiding Statistical Biases}
\label{sec:biases}

This section gives the practical mitigation for the biases that arise when
working with merged multi-agency catalogues.  The underlying mechanisms are
described in Section~\ref{sec:biasmech}; here we recap each only briefly and
concentrate on the corrective steps and how CofC supports them.  These biases
are not unique to CofC; they affect any merged catalogue analysis.

\subsection{Magnitude completeness bias}

\textbf{Problem.} Fitting $b$ below $M_c$ underestimates it, because the
incomplete sample is deficient at small magnitudes (Section~\ref{sec:biasmech}).
Completeness also varies in space: a merged catalogue can be complete to low
magnitudes where one agency's network is dense and only to much higher ones
offshore or at depth.

\textbf{Guidance.}
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
  \item Always estimate $M_c$ before fitting~$b$.  Use the CofC MAXC or
    goodness-of-fit estimate as a starting point but verify it by eye on the FMD
    plot, and check that $\hat{b}$ is stable as the cut-off is raised (the
    magnitude filter sets an explicit cut-off).  In the synthetic example
    (Section~\ref{sec:example}) both automatic estimates ($M$1.7 and $M$1.9)
    lie well below the $M$2.7 from which $\hat{b}$ is stable.
  \item Set the lower magnitude cut-off slightly above $M_c$ (e.g.\ $M_c + 0.1$)
    as a practical conservatism against partial completeness in the transition
    zone.
  \item After merging catalogues with different $M_c$ values, use at least the
    \emph{higher} of the two as the common cut-off.  This is necessary but not
    sufficient: in the synthetic example the higher of the two catalogue
    estimates ($M$1.8, or $M$1.9 by the goodness-of-fit test) is still below the
    merged catalogue's completeness, because completeness also varies within each
    network.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Magnitude heterogeneity bias}

\textbf{Problem.}  Mixing magnitude scales artificially broadens the FMD and can
shift the apparent $b$-value, in part because different scales saturate at
different magnitudes (Section~\ref{sec:biasmech}).

\textbf{Guidance.}
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
  \item Inspect the magnitude-type distribution before fitting: the Analysis
    page tabulates the magnitude types of the events under analysis, warns when
    the Gutenberg-Richter or $M_c$ sample mixes scale families, and can restrict
    the analysis to chosen types.
  \item Convert to a single preferred scale (ideally Mw) using empirical
    regressions \citep{das2011,hanks1979}, preferably orthogonal rather than
    ordinary least-squares fits, to avoid conversion bias
    \citep{castellaro2006,lolli2012}, where possible.
  \item If conversion is not possible, split the catalogue by magnitude type
    and fit each subset separately.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Network coverage and azimuthal gap bias}

\textbf{Problem.}  Poorly located events from sparse-network regions can bias
statistical distributions if they concentrate at particular magnitudes or
depths (Section~\ref{sec:biasmech}).  Removing them is not free: because location
quality improves with magnitude, a quality filter removes small events
preferentially.

\textbf{Guidance.}
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
  \item Filter on quality score (e.g., $Q \geq 45$, grade C or better) or
    azimuthal gap (e.g., gap $\leq 170^{\circ}$, in the page's $10^{\circ}$
    steps) before location-sensitive analyses such as spatial mapping.  Records
    the reporting agency flagged as duplicate, not existing, or not locatable are
    excluded from the Analysis page by default.
  \item Before fitting $b$ to a quality-filtered catalogue, compare the
    frequency-magnitude distributions or $b$-values with and without the filter
    at the same cut-off: in the synthetic example a $Q \geq 70$ filter keeps 58\%
    of the events of $M$1.5--2.0 but 83\% of those of $M$3.5--4.0, and lowers
    $\hat{b}$ by 0.06 even above the completeness magnitude.
  \item Check the spatial distribution of excluded events; geographic
    concentration may indicate a bias rather than a data problem.
  \item Use the CofC map coloured by quality grade or by azimuthal gap to
    identify network-coverage artefacts.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Aftershock clustering and GR analysis}

\textbf{Problem.}  GR analysis assumes independent, Poissonian arrivals.
Aftershock sequences cluster in space, time, and magnitude, violating this
assumption and shifting the estimated $b$.  Declustering shifts it too: window
methods remove small independent events more readily than large ones
\citep{mizrahi2021}.  In the synthetic example of Section~\ref{sec:example}, where
the clustered population was assigned a higher $b$ by construction, the
quality-filtered catalogue gives $\hat{b} = 1.00$ above $M$2.7 before
declustering and $0.88$ after, below the planted background value of $1.00$,
because the windows also remove 1\,617 small independent events.  The
declustered value also depends on the window convention: a symmetric window
gives $0.82$ on the same catalogue (Section~\ref{sec:decluster}), a difference of
about four formal $\sigma_b$, so the convention must be reported alongside any
declustered $b$.

\textbf{Guidance.}
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
  \item Decluster before analyses that assume Poissonian seismicity.  The
    Gardner-Knopoff method \citep{gardner1974} implemented in CofC is the
    standard choice for tectonic seismicity; Reasenberg
    \citeyearpar{reasenberg1985} is preferable for swarm-dominated sequences.
  \item For $b$-values, compare the original and the declustered catalogue:
    export the catalogue with the Gardner-Knopoff tags and fit both, treating the
    difference as a measure of the preprocessing uncertainty rather than as a
    correction.
  \item Report the declustering algorithm and parameters in publications so
    results are reproducible.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Duplicate double-counting}

\textbf{Problem.}  Merging without duplicate removal counts each shared
earthquake once per report, inflating $N(M)$ wherever the catalogues overlap,
raising the estimated $a$-value, and distorting rate estimates.  In the
synthetic example, concatenating the two catalogues instead of merging them
would raise the number of events above $M$2.7 from 8\,556 to 13\,312 (56\%
more) and the $a$-value from 6.80 to 6.87; because the shared events are the
larger ones that both networks record, it would also lower $\hat{b}$, from 1.06 to
1.02.  The Analysis page therefore withholds the $b$-value, $M_c$, rates and
moment totals for a set pooled from several catalogues.

\textbf{Guidance.}
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
  \item Always merge catalogues before statistical analysis; never concatenate
    raw files.
  \item Inspect the merge preview and summary (duplicate count, groups flagged
    as failing a validity gate or ambiguous, strategy applied) and satisfy
    yourself that the matching thresholds are physically appropriate for your
    region and magnitude range.
  \item For teleseismic analysis, increase the distance threshold (e.g., to
    100~km) to account for larger location scatter at global distances.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Temporal non-stationarity}

\textbf{Problem.}  Network upgrades, station outages, or changes in analysis
procedures alter $M_c$ over time.  A single $b$ fitted over a long catalogue
period conflates eras with different completeness.

\textbf{Guidance.}
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
  \item Use the CofC Temporal tab to identify sudden jumps in the rate of events
    above $M_c$ or in the magnitude--time distribution, likely indicators of
    network changes.
  \item Split the catalogue at breakpoints and estimate $b$ for each period
    separately.
  \item The \citet{mignan2012} review provides detailed methods for
    time-varying $M_c$ analysis.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Discussion and Conclusions}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Relation to existing approaches}

CofC addresses a gap that is simultaneously methodological and
infrastructural.  The methodological gap is well documented in the CORSSA
literature \citep{naylor2010, mignan2012, vanstiphout2012}: the biases
described in Section~\ref{sec:biases} are known, quantifiable, and avoidable,
yet they continue to appear in published seismicity studies because the
corrective steps (enforcing $M_c$, homogenising magnitude scales, declustering,
and quality filtering) are each individually inconvenient to apply and are
rarely performed together in a reproducible workflow.  The infrastructural gap,
that no single production-ready tool spans multi-source ingest, merging, quality
scoring, and the statistical workflow, is set out against existing tools in
Section~\ref{sec:existing} and Table~\ref{tab:comparison}.  What CofC adds beyond
that comparison is to close the loop end to end, from multi-source ingest through
duplicate removal, quality scoring, declustering, and GR fitting, within a single
persistent web interface that preserves provenance at every step and requires no
local installation.

\subsection{Quality scoring and event selection}

The quality-scoring system (equation~\eqref{eq:quality}) is designed to be
interpretable: each sub-score corresponds to a well-understood determinant of
solution quality, grounded in the seismological literature.  Azimuthal gap
is among the principal network-geometry predictors of
horizontal location error \citep{bondar2004}; used-station and
used-phase counts further constrain the solution;
location and magnitude uncertainties are direct quantitative measures of
solution precision.  By making these criteria explicit and combining them into
a single 0--100 index, CofC provides a reproducible basis for quality-based
event selection that does not depend on subjective judgement of agency
provenance.  Because the sub-scores scale with network density, however, this
ranking favours densely recorded events and is not network-neutral
(Section~\ref{sec:quality}); and because they scale with the number of recording
stations, it also favours larger events, so a threshold on $Q$ is a
magnitude-dependent selection (Section~\ref{sec:example}).

An alternative quality-filtering approach common in the literature is to
apply hard thresholds on individual parameters (e.g., retain only events with
gap $< 180^{\circ}$ and $\sigma_M < 0.3$).  Such thresholds are easier to
report in a methods section but are less flexible: a threshold approach
discards events entirely, whereas the quality score allows users to weight
the trade-off between sample size and data quality continuously.  CofC
exposes both approaches: the catalogue page and the filter API select events by
a minimum quality score or by per-field limits (azimuthal gap, station and
phase counts, RMS residual, and the location, depth, time and magnitude
uncertainties), and the Analysis page filters by $Q$ and by azimuthal gap.

\subsection{Towards reproducible seismicity analysis}

A persistent challenge in observational seismology is that two researchers
applying the same analysis method to nominally the same dataset can obtain
different results, because their preprocessing choices (format conversion,
duplicate removal, $M_c$ cut-off, declustering algorithm and parameters,
quality filters) differ in ways that are rarely fully reported
\citep{schorlemmer2007}.  CofC addresses this at the infrastructure
level: every catalogue stored in the platform carries a provenance record
(source catalogue IDs and contributing reports per event, merge strategy and
parameters, quality score) and a version.  Every export includes this record
together with the catalogue version, a checksum of the exported rows, the filter
applied and, when requested, the declustering algorithm, its parameters and
per-event tags, and the QuakeML export format \citep{schorlemmer2011} provides a
standard container for sharing it.  The platform thus encourages a
workflow in which the preprocessing choices are not buried in bespoke scripts
but are captured as persistent, shareable catalogue objects.

\subsection{Limitations and future work}

\textbf{$M_c$ estimation.}  The MAXC method \citep{wiemer2000} implemented in
CofC is fast and requires no assumptions about the GR model, but
\citet{woessner2005} showed that it systematically underestimates $M_c$ by
$\sim 0.1$--$0.2$ magnitude units in typical network conditions.  The $+0.2$
correction applied by default mitigates this but does not eliminate it, and the
goodness-of-fit test offered as an alternative shares a deeper limitation: both
estimate one $M_c$ for the events under analysis.  On a merged catalogue whose
completeness varies in space, the worked example shows both falling well below
the magnitude from which $b$ is stable (Section~\ref{sec:example}).  The
Entire-Magnitude-Range method \citep{woessner2005}, a $b$-value-stability
estimate \citep{caogao2002}, spatially varying $M_c$ \citep{schorlemmer2008},
and, for time-varying $M_c$, the bootstrap approach described by
\citet{mignan2012} are planned.

\textbf{Spatial analysis.}  CofC currently provides only global (single
value) $b$-value estimates.  Spatial mapping of $b$ \citep{wiemer2000,
schorlemmer2005}, of $M_c$ \citep{schorlemmer2008}, and of seismicity rates on a grid or along a fault
structure requires server-side spatial indexing and is targeted for a future
release.  Similarly, ETAS parameter fitting \citep{ogata1988} and
forecast evaluation using the CSEP framework and its consistency tests
\citep{schorlemmer2007,zechar2010} are
not yet implemented.

\textbf{Scale.}  Catalogues larger than $\sim 10^6$ events require
server-side pre-aggregation (e.g., hexagonal binning for the map view,
streaming computation for GR fits) for smooth browser rendering.  Mongo\-DB's
aggregation pipeline and server-side sampling, already used for the API
pagination layer, will be extended to support these workflows.

\textbf{Magnitude homogenisation.}  CofC displays the magnitude type of
each event, tabulates the types under analysis, and warns when a fit mixes
scales, but it does not yet apply empirical inter-scale conversion
automatically.  Given the magnitude
bias this can introduce \citep{marzocchi2003, tinti1987}, automated
conversion to a unified scale (ideally $M_w$) using orthogonal regressions
such as those of \citet{das2011} and \citet{lolli2012} is a high-priority
addition; database-scale homogenisation workflows such as that of
\citet{weatherill2016} provide a template.

\subsection{Conclusions}

We have presented CofC, a browser-based platform for earthquake catalogue
management, merging, quality scoring, and seismological analysis.  The core
contribution is infrastructural: a single persistent interface that integrates
format ingestion, duplicate detection, transparent quality scoring, and
provenance-preserving export, the steps that are individually inconvenient
yet collectively determine the validity of downstream statistical analysis.
Using a fully synthetic two-catalogue New Zealand example processed end to end
by the platform's own engine, we illustrated the workflow and quantified what it
exposes: the matcher pairs 93\% of the earthquakes both agencies report, with
98\% of its pairs correct; the quality index ranks the offshore, high-gap
population lowest, and a quality threshold removes 95\% of it; and three
preprocessing choices move the $b$-value by more than its formal error, namely a
single global $M_c$ on a catalogue whose completeness varies in space, a
magnitude-selective quality filter ($-0.06$), and window declustering ($-0.12$).
Once the cut-off lies above the merged catalogue's completeness, the estimator
recovers the planted $b = 1.00$ of the independent events ($1.01 \pm 0.01$).
These figures characterise the controlled synthetic inputs and the software's
behaviour on them, not real New Zealand seismicity.
The priority next steps are validation on heterogeneous real catalogues,
measurement of duplicate-detection precision and recall, and calibration of the
quality score against independent benchmarks.  By making preprocessing choices explicit and exportable,
CofC supports the shift towards reproducible, openly documented seismological
practice advocated by the CORSSA community \citep{naylor2010} and the FAIR data
principles \citep{wilkinson2016}.

The platform is actively developed; community contributions, feature requests,
and bug reports are welcomed via the GitHub issue tracker.
